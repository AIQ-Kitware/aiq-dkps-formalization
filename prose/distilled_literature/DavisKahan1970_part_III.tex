\section{Completion standard}

The source explicitly says that all four headline theorems apply in infinite as
well as finite dimensions and are asserted for arbitrary unitary-invariant
norms. Its standing subject is a bounded Hermitian operator on a separable
Hilbert space, while unbounded self-adjoint operators are covered by extensions
whose extra work is concentrated in Theorem 5.2 and the Appendix to Section 6.
Consequently, proof of the finite quartet is not proof of the paper.

A paper-completion claim requires, at minimum:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item a source-faithful generalized unbounded single-angle theorem with
explicit domains, bounded residual, source gap configurations, and the stated
unitary-invariant norm scope, together with bounded and finite specializations;
\item source-faithful Hilbert-space versions of the remaining main theorem
families, in residual and perturbation forms and with every unbounded extension
supplied by the source;
\item the source unitary-invariant norm scope, represented by honest
operator-ideal or finiteness hypotheses rather than finite singular-value
lists alone;
\item the two-subspace and direct-rotation theory of Sections 1--4;
\item the source Sylvester theorems, including the boundedness/domain cases;
\item the Appendix to Section 6 and its unbounded self-adjoint extensions;
\item the Section 8 continuation, branch-selection, spectral-subspace, and
repulsion results;
\item the sharpness/equality statements and a theorem-number audit of all
numbered results.
\end{enumerate}

The current finite package remains a first-class deliverable because it has
weaker foundational dependencies, supports rectangular maps and finite UI
norms directly, and is useful for computation and applications.

The paper is not one theorem with four cosmetic variants. It explicitly emphasizes that none of the four main results implies the other three. Their gap configurations and off-diagonal hypotheses differ, and their sharp conclusions answer different geometric questions. A source-faithful API should retain the paper's Hilbert-space ambient generality and four distinct statement shapes. Finite declarations should be labeled as specializations, even when they prove stronger rectangular or lower-frame variants within finite dimensions.

\section{Standing assumptions from the transcription}

\sourceanchor{Introduction; Section 1; Section 2 immediately following the four theorem statements.}

The transcription fixes several scope facts that must govern theorem names and
completion claims:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item $\Hsp$ is a separable Hilbert space, real or complex, with finite
 dimensionality only a special case.
\item The standing main-body subject is a bounded Hermitian operator $A$ and a
 Hermitian modification $A+H$.
\item The results and proofs also apply to unbounded self-adjoint $A$ when the
 domain of $H$ contains the domain of $A$; useful bounds require the relevant
 perturbation or residual to be bounded.
\item All four headline theorems are stated as applicable in infinite as well
 as finite dimensions.
\item All four headline bounds are asserted for arbitrary unitary-invariant
 norms. For noncompact operators the source notes that singular-value lists may
 need to be replaced by spectral multiplicity language and that some norm
 statements can be vacuous when the norm is not finite.
\item The additional work needed for the unbounded scope is concentrated in
 Theorem 5.2 and the Appendix to Section 6, rather than being absent from the
 paper.
\end{enumerate}

A Lean theorem with a finite-dimensional assumption can be paper-inspired,
paper-exact after explicit specialization, or stronger in a finite rectangular
direction. It is not the unqualified paper theorem merely because its formula
and constant match the displayed source inequality.

\section{Section 1: two block decompositions and the residual}

\sourceanchor{Section 1, equations (1.1)--(1.9).}

Let $A=A^*$ act on a Hilbert space $\Hsp$, let $P$ be a reducing orthogonal projection for $A$, and write
\[
  \Hsp=P\Hsp\oplus P^\perp\Hsp.
\]
Relative to isometries $E_0,E_1$ onto these two summands, the source represents
\[
  A\sim
  \begin{pmatrix}A_0&0\\0&A_1\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  H\sim
  \begin{pmatrix}H_0&B^*\\B&H_1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
A second reducing projection $Q$ for $A+H$ gives isometries $F_0,F_1$ and another diagonal representation
\[
  A+H\sim
  \begin{pmatrix}\widehat A_0&0\\0&\widehat A_1\end{pmatrix}.
\]
No basis of eigenvectors is required. This is already the coordinate-free subspace formulation that the Lean development should preserve.

For numerical approximation, $E_0$ is an isometry whose range is a trial subspace, and $A_0$ is a chosen Ritz or trial operator. The residual is
\begin{equation}
  R=(A+H)E_0-E_0A_0.
  \tag{DK-1.8}
\end{equation}
If $A_0=E_0^*(A+H)E_0$, then $E_0^*R=0$; in the source block notation this is $H_0=0$. Thus the tan theorem's off-diagonal hypothesis is the Rayleigh--Ritz condition, not an arbitrary technical decoration.

\subsection{Unitarily invariant norms}

\sourceanchor{Section 1, equations (1.9)--(1.13).}

The source works systematically with norms invariant under left and right unitary multiplication. It recalls:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=2em]
\item compatibility with contractions on both sides;
\item dependence only on singular values;
\item the Ky Fan prefix norms
\[
  \norm{K}_{(r)}=s_1(K)+\cdots+s_r(K);
\]
\item Fan dominance: comparison of every Ky Fan prefix is equivalent to comparison in every unitarily invariant norm.
\end{itemize}
This is why the Sylvester lemma in Section 5 is the central analytic engine: once a suitable inequality is proved for every unitarily invariant norm, the geometric angle operators inherit it without choosing coordinates.

\section{Sections 1 and 3: principal angles, sines, and projectors}

\sourceanchor{Section 1 around equations (1.14)--(1.18), and Section 3.}

For two subspaces $P\Hsp$ and $Q\Hsp$ of compatible dimension, the singular values of the cross-compressions determine their canonical angles. In a principal two-plane one has the model
\[
 P=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q=\begin{pmatrix}
   \cos^2\theta&\cos\theta\sin\theta\\
   \cos\theta\sin\theta&\sin^2\theta
 \end{pmatrix}.
\]
The off-diagonal maps have singular value $\sin\theta\cos\theta$, while
\[
  \norm{P-Q}_{\op}=\sin\theta_{
  \max}.
\]
At the operator level, the source packages the angles in a positive operator $\Theta$ and defines $\sin\Theta$, $\cos\Theta$, $\tan\Theta$, and their double-angle analogues by functional calculus.

The finite projector theorem is a direct companion to the sine theorem because the nonzero singular values of $P-Q$ are the directed sine singular values, with the usual zero or multiplicity adjustments. In Lean this relationship is represented by declarations around
\code{opNorm_projection_sub_eq_opNorm_sinThetaMap},
\code{norm_starProjection_sub_eq_max}, and the directed sine maps.

\section{Section 4: the direct rotation}

\sourceanchor{Section 4, extremal properties of the direct rotation.}

The direct rotation $U$ is the distinguished unitary carrying $P\Hsp$ to $Q\Hsp$ while rotating each principal two-plane through its canonical angle and fixing the common parts appropriately. Formally it has the trigonometric form
\[
  U=\cos\Theta+J\sin\Theta=\exp(J\Theta),
\]
where $J$ is the quarter-turn partial isometry on the generic two-plane component.

The paper proves that the direct rotation is extremal among unitaries intertwining $P$ and $Q$: it is closest to the identity in the natural spectral sense. Section 4 is geometrically important but is not used to prove all four inequalities. This separation matters for formalization. The classical inequalities can be established from projection geometry and Sylvester equations before a complete reusable API for extremal direct rotations exists.

The extremality results must be read proposition by proposition.  In particular, the printed Proposition~4.4 is false at the stated scope: the repository retains the printed proposition as a formal predicate and proves its negation by an explicit finite-dimensional counterexample.  Thus faithful formal treatment of Section~4 includes a refutation rather than a proof of that printed assertion.

\section{Section 2: the four source statements}

\sourceanchor{Section 2, ``Statement of the theorems.''}

Section~2 states each theorem in two related geometries.  The directed angle
$\Theta_0$ compares the trial space with the chosen reducing space and is paired
with the residual $R$.  The ambient angle $\Theta$ compares the two full
orthogonal decompositions and is paired with the perturbation $H$.  The second
conclusion is therefore not obtained merely by replacing the symbol $R$ by $H$:
the angle object changes as well.

\subsection{The sin $\Theta$ theorem}

Suppose the trial spectral set lies in an interval $[\beta,\alpha]$ and the complementary exact spectral set lies outside the enlarged interval
\[
  (\beta-\delta,\alpha+\delta).
\]
Then, for every unitarily invariant norm,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\sin\Theta_0}
  \le \norm{R}.
  \tag{DK-sin}\label{DK-sin}
\end{equation}
The source immediately notes that this interval/exterior statement is not the definitive formulation; Theorem~6.1 generalizes it by allowing a non-isometric trial map and unequal dimensions while retaining the same interval/exterior spectral separation.

The important asymmetry is that the unwanted exact spectrum may occur on both sides of the trial spectrum. This broad gap geometry is one reason the sine theorem is more generally applicable than the tangent theorem.

\subsection{The tan $\Theta$ theorem}

Assume the trial spectrum lies in $[\beta,\alpha]$, the complementary exact spectrum lies entirely on one side, for example in $[\alpha+\delta,\infty)$, and the Rayleigh--Ritz condition $H_0=0$ holds.  The printed theorem has two conclusions:
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\tan\Theta_0}\le \norm{R},
  \qquad
  \delta\,\norm{\tan\Theta}\le \norm{H}.
  \tag{DK-tan}
\end{equation}
The first is the directed residual statement; the second is the ambient perturbation statement.  Both are asserted for every unitarily invariant norm.

\subsection{The sin $2\Theta$ theorem}

Here the gap is between the two diagonal blocks of the perturbed operator in the $Q$-decomposition.  Under the source interval/exterior hypothesis,
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\sin 2\Theta_0}\le 2\norm{R},
  \qquad
  \delta\,\norm{\sin 2\Theta}\le 2\norm{H}.
  \tag{DK-sin2}\label{DK-sin2}
\end{equation}
Section~7 proves the ambient estimate first by reflection and then sharpens the directed residual conclusion to
\[
  \delta\,\norm{\sin 2\Theta_0}\le \norm{B}\le \norm{R},
\]
so the directed residual form does not pay the ambient factor two.

\paragraph{Source-check note.}
The maintained modernized transcription records this factor-one residual step
exactly as displayed above, while the Section~2 theorem itself prints the
factor-two bound.  Repository repair notes have questioned whether the
factor-one line is a transcription/source inconsistency because
$H-XHX$ doubles the off-diagonal block.  The exact-source register therefore
preserves the maintained transcription verbatim rather than silently choosing a
normalization; a final adversarial audit should compare this passage against the
original page image before treating the sharper factor as an independent source
obligation.

\subsection{The tan $2\Theta$ theorem}

Assume the two diagonal spectral blocks $A_0,A_1$ of the unperturbed operator are separated on one side by $\delta$, and assume the perturbation is fully off-diagonal,
\[
  H_0=0,\qquad H_1=0.
\]
For an arbitrary reducing subspace of $A+H$, the printed theorem concludes
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\tan 2\Theta_0}\le 2\norm{R},
  \qquad
  \delta\,\norm{\tan 2\Theta}\le 2\norm{H}.
  \tag{DK-tan2}\label{DK-tan2}
\end{equation}
No quarter-angle branch or spectral placement assumption on the $Q$-blocks of
$A+H$ appears in this Section~2 statement.  Section~7 derives the necessary
pointwise exclusion of the $\pi/4$ pole from the gap during the proof.  The
selected branch $\Theta<\pi/4$ belongs instead to the additional spectral
selection theorem in Section~8.

\section{Section 5: the source's analytic engine $AX-XB=C$}

\sourceanchor{Section 5, especially Theorems 5.1 and 5.2.}

Let $A$ and $B$ be Hermitian operators on possibly different Hilbert spaces, and let $X$ be rectangular. The equation
\begin{equation}
  AX-XB=C
  \tag{DK-Syl}
\end{equation}
is the common algebraic core of the angle estimates.

\subsection{Theorem 5.1: the Banach inverse-norm estimate}

Theorem~5.1 deliberately leaves Hilbert-space spectral geometry behind.  Let
$\mathcal X$ and $\mathcal Y$ be Banach spaces, let $B$ act on $\mathcal X$ and
$A$ on $\mathcal Y$, and suppose
\[
  \norm{B}\le \alpha,
  \qquad
  \norm{A^{-1}}\le (\alpha+\delta)^{-1},
  \qquad \alpha\ge0,\quad \delta>0.
\]
For any norm on cross-operators compatible with the bound norms, the equation
\[
  AX-XB=C
\]
implies
\begin{equation}
  \norm{C}\ge \delta\norm{X}.
  \tag{DK-5.1}
\end{equation}
Indeed, compatibility gives $\norm{XB}\le\alpha\norm{X}$ while the inverse
bound gives $\norm{AX}\ge(\alpha+\delta)\norm{X}$, and the triangle inequality
finishes the estimate.  The source immediately notes that the roles of $A$ and
$B$ may be interchanged.  It also remarks that the same statement and proof
allow a densely defined unbounded $A$ when its bounded inverse is defined on all
of $\mathcal Y$.

This elementary inverse-norm theorem is distinct from Theorem~5.2, which is the
self-adjoint ordered-gap extension used by the later angle arguments.

\subsection{Theorem 5.2: the unbounded ordered extension}

The maintained transcription identifies Theorem 5.2 as the domain-aware
extension of the ordered constant-one Sylvester estimate to self-adjoint blocks
that may be unbounded.  The bounded rectangular maps $X$ and $C$ satisfy the
closed equation $AX-XB=C$ on the domain of $B$, including the transport
condition $X(\mathcal D(B))\subseteq\mathcal D(A)$.  The two diagonal blocks
obey opposite semibounds separated by $\delta$.

The source proof cuts one unbounded block down by bounded spectral projections,
uses the ordered finite Ky Fan inequalities for the resulting bounded
truncations, and then lets the cutoff radius tend to infinity.  Two distinct
formal seams are therefore essential:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item spectral cutoff preserves the domain-aware Sylvester equation and the
ordered form bounds;
\item every finite Ky Fan gauge converges under the strongly convergent
orthogonal cutoffs, allowing the cutoff inequalities to pass to $X$ and $C$.
\end{enumerate}
Fan dominance then converts the family of finite Ky Fan inequalities into the
claimed arbitrary-unitarily-invariant ideal bound.

This theorem is not the later arbitrary-separated-spectrum estimate with
constant $\pi/2$.  That Fourier-multiplier result is useful additional
infrastructure, but it is not the source of the ordered unbounded sine theorem.

\section{Section 6: the single-angle proofs}

\sourceanchor{Theorem 6.1, the generalized sin theorem; the subsequent tan theorem proof.}

\subsection{The generalized sine theorem}

Theorem~6.1 retains the interval/exterior separation of the original sine theorem but generalizes the geometry.  The exact decomposition is still described by isometries $F_0,F_1$ with
\[
  F_0F_0^*+F_1F_1^*=1,
\]
while the trial map $E_0$ need no longer be isometric: it is only required to satisfy the lower-frame inequality
\[
  E_0^*E_0\ge \varepsilon^2,
  \qquad \varepsilon>0.
\]
The dimensions of $\Range(E_0)$ and $\Range(F_0)$ need not agree.  If the spectrum of $A_0$ lies in $[\beta,\alpha]$ and that of $\Lambda_1$ lies outside $(\beta-\delta,\alpha+\delta)$, or conversely, then for every unitarily invariant norm
\begin{equation}
  \delta\varepsilon\,\norm{\sin\Theta_0}\le \norm{R}.
  \tag{DK-6.1}
\end{equation}
Here $\sin\Theta_0$ may be any operator having the singular values of the directed cross-projection $P Q^\perp$.

The proof has the same analytic core as the original sine theorem.  The residual identity gives
\[
  R^*F_1=E_0^*F_1\Lambda_1-A_0E_0^*F_1,
\]
Theorem~5.1 bounds $\norm{E_0^*F_1}$ by $\delta^{-1}\norm R$, and the lower-frame assumption converts the cross map to the projector sine with loss exactly $\varepsilon^{-1}$.

\subsection{The tangent theorem}

For the tangent estimate, the off-diagonal condition allows the residual equation to be divided by the cosine block. In principal-coordinate notation the cross block has the form
\[
  S C^{-1}=\tan\Theta.
\]
The source's one-sided spectral placement ensures the relevant cosine is invertible and gives another ordered Sylvester inequality. The gain from $\sin\Theta$ to $\tan\Theta$ is paid for by the stronger spectral orientation and by the Rayleigh--Ritz condition.

A modern pole-free proof should not simply write $C^{-1}$ before proving that no angle equals $\pi/2$. Nakatsukasa and Motovilov clarify how the required graph property follows from spectral placement.  The elementary \code{tan_theta_le} theorem remains useful as a coordinate-free operator-norm proof with substantially lighter singular-value dependencies.

\subsubsection*{Source-audit correction: Theorem 6.3 is directed, not symmetrically acute}

An earlier version of this distillation made a serious transcription error.  It
replaced the paper's strict inequality
\[
  \dim \mathcal X(E_0)<\dim \mathcal X(F_0)
\]
by an abstract isometric embedding of the smaller trial space into the larger
exact space, and then treated that embedding as if it implied the symmetric
Davis--Kahan acute relation between the two ambient subspaces.  That implication
is false: a proper subspace embeds isometrically into a larger subspace while
the reverse directed projection can have a nontrivial kernel.

The printed theorem does not make this inference.  It defines the directed sine
data from the singular values of
\[
  E_0^*F_1,
\]
or equivalently of the adjoint cross block $F_1^*E_0$, and defines
$\tan\Theta_0$ by applying tangent to that same singular-value list.  The
conclusion concerns only this directed rectangular tangent representative.  It
does not assert a direct rotation of the full unequal-dimensional pair and does
not require \code{IsAcute Z V}.

The false symmetric formulation is now guarded by a compiled counterexample
in
\code{DavisKahan.Sources.DavisKahan1970.Audits.Theorem63Distillation}.
A second audit observation closes the bounded source theorem: the paper works
throughout in a separable Hilbert space, so its strict Hilbert-dimension
inequality forces the smaller trial-coordinate space to be finite-dimensional.
Thus the source proof requires finite singular vectors only on the trial side;
the ambient and exact spectral subspaces may remain infinite-dimensional.
The production module
\code{DavisKahan.TanTheta.Theorem63FiniteSource} implements equation (6.6),
all Ky Fan prefix inequalities, finite-rank tail stabilization, and the
Fan-dominance promotion to arbitrary supported unitary-invariant ideal gauges.
The equal-dimension noncompact tangent theorem and the arbitrary-ideal
unbounded Appendix passage remain separate obligations.

\section{Section 7: reflection and the double-angle theorems}

\sourceanchor{Section 7, proof of the sin $2\theta$ and tan $2\theta$ theorems.}

Let
\[
  J_P=2P-I
\]
be the reflection across $P\Hsp$. Conjugating by $J_P$ leaves the diagonal blocks unchanged and flips the sign of the off-diagonal blocks. If
\[
  \widetilde A=J_P(A+H)J_P,
\]
then the subspace $J_PQ\Hsp$ makes angle $2\Theta$ with $Q\Hsp$ on the canonical two-plane component. This ``mirror'' converts the double-angle problem into a single-angle problem for two related operators.

For the sine theorem, the source first applies the single-angle theorem to the symmetric pair and obtains the ambient estimate
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\sin 2\Theta}
  \le \norm{H-XHX}
  \le 2\norm{H}.
  \tag{DK-7.5}
\end{equation}
It then rewrites the same comparison in off-diagonal block form and, in the maintained transcription, records the sharper directed residual conclusion
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,\norm{\sin 2\Theta_0}\le \norm{B}\le \norm{R}.
  \tag{DK-7-directed}
\end{equation}
This line is stronger by a factor of two than the directed statement printed in the Section~2 theorem summary.  The exact-source register therefore preserves both passages verbatim rather than silently identifying them.  The discrepancy should be checked against the original page image during the independent audit; until then the formalization ledger must say explicitly which source passage each Lean declaration certifies.

The ambient factor two has a transparent origin: $H-XHX$ is twice the off-diagonal contribution.  Because the sine theorem tolerates spectrum on both sides, no full off-diagonal assumption is needed.

For the tangent theorem, full off-diagonality makes the reflected operator the opposite perturbation of the same diagonal $A$:
\[
  J_P(A+H)J_P=A-H.
\]
The tangent theorem can then be applied to the pair $A+H$ and $A-H$, yielding \eqref{DK-tan2}. The branch analysis is deferred to Section 8.

The finite Lean implementation mirrors this structure through projection reflections and theorems such as
\code{sin_two_theta_reflection_le},
\code{sin_two_theta_starProjection_le}, and
\code{tan_two_theta_norm_sub_le}.

\section{Section 8: branch selection and the quarter-turn barrier}

\sourceanchor{Theorems 8.1 and 8.2.}

Theorem 8.1 interprets the tan $2\Theta$ hypotheses geometrically and proves that the selected subspaces lie in the acute branch:
\[
  \norm{\Theta}_{\op}<\frac{\pi}{4}.
\]
This is not recoverable from a bound on $\tan 2\Theta$ alone, because the tangent has a pole at $\pi/4$ and changes branch afterward. A source-faithful formal theorem must therefore carry either an explicit branch conclusion or hypotheses strong enough to derive it.

Theorem 8.2 treats spectral continuation under a small perturbation and identifies the correct perturbed spectral cluster. This is the source-level origin of canonical spectral-subspace wrappers: an abstract invariant subspace inequality becomes useful only after proving that the invariant subspace is the spectral subspace selected by the intended interval.

\section{Sharpness and planar models}

\sourceanchor{Section 2 sharpness remarks and Section 9.}

Each constant is already sharp in a two-dimensional principal plane. A typical model takes
\[
  A=\begin{pmatrix}-a&0\\0&a\end{pmatrix}
\]
and rotates or perturbs its eigenspaces by an angle $\theta$. Direct sums of such planes can make several singular-angle coordinates simultaneously extremal. This explains why equality is meaningful for arbitrary unitarily invariant norms, not only for the operator norm.

The formalization should retain small explicit equality models as regression tests. They catch errors in the factor two, in the orientation of the residual, and in the definition of the directed sine map.

\section{Section 9: exact structure of the numerical example}

\sourceanchor{Section 9, equations (9.1)--(9.11).}

The example starts from the self-adjoint closure of the fourth derivative on
$L^2(0,1)$ with free-end boundary conditions
$u''(0)=u'''(0)=u''(1)=u'''(1)=0$, perturbed by multiplication by
$\varepsilon t$, where $0<\varepsilon<100$.  The two-dimensional trial space is
the null eigenspace of the unperturbed operator, spanned by two explicit
orthonormal linear functions.  The third unperturbed eigenvalue is greater
than $500$, providing the comparison gap.

For the zero comparison matrix the residual Gram matrix is
\[
  R^*R=\frac{\varepsilon^2}{30}
  \begin{pmatrix}11-\sqrt{75}&-1\\-1&11+\sqrt{75}\end{pmatrix},
\]
with eigenvalues $\varepsilon^2(11\pm\sqrt{76})/30$.  The source derives the
operator-norm and two-singular-value consequences (9.1)--(9.4) by applying the
sine and sine-double-angle theorems.

The Rayleigh--Ritz comparison matrix is diagonal:
\[
  \widehat A_0=\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
  \begin{pmatrix}1-1/\sqrt3&0\\0&1+1/\sqrt3\end{pmatrix}.
\]
The orthogonal residual satisfies
\[
  \widehat R^*\widehat R=\frac{\varepsilon^2}{30}
  \begin{pmatrix}1&-1\\-1&1\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \|\widehat R\|_{\op}=\|\widehat R\|_{(2)}=\varepsilon/\sqrt{15}.
\]
Equations (9.5)--(9.7) then follow from the tangent and tangent-double-angle
theorems.  The decimal bounds in the paper should be formalized as corollaries
of exact radical and rational inequalities.

The second half of the example has three logically separate components:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item a comparison with Weinberger's lower-bound method through a concrete
  $3\times3$ matrix and equation (9.8);
\item an infinite-dimensional example showing that a good Rayleigh quotient
  need not give a finite residual when the trial vector is outside the
  operator domain;
\item a Schur-complement reduction, equations (9.9)--(9.11), followed by
  tangent and double-angle bounds for identifying individual eigenvectors
  inside the two-eigenvalue cluster.
\end{enumerate}

The repository now formalizes both the finite block computations and the free-beam analytic model, including the exact Gram matrices, numerical inequalities, Schur-complement step, individual-angle bounds, and real/complex model variants.  Those source-facing modules are indexed under \code{DavisKahan/Sources/DavisKahan1970/Section9/}.

\section{What should not be conflated with this paper}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=2em]
\item Completion of a finite-dimensional quartet is not completion of the 1970 paper. It is a specialization with valuable weaker foundations.
\item Theorem 6.3 is a directed unequal-dimension statement.  A Hilbert-dimension embedding must never be upgraded to symmetric acuteness or to a full direct-rotation tangent operator; the source uses the singular values of the rectangular cross block $E_0^*F_1$.
\item The arbitrary-separated-spectrum Sylvester estimate with sharp constant $\pi/2$ is later theory. It is useful infrastructure but is not the analytic root of the four source statements.
\item Exact finite reciprocal orbit interpolation is stronger than any of the four classical conclusions. In particular, a generic undoubled real certificate at mass $\pi/2$ is false and must not be installed as a hidden premise of this API.
\item Finite-dimensional tangent specializations, branch-selected tangent theorems, branch-free double-angle statements, and unbounded ideal-gauge extensions are distinct theorem surfaces even when they share constants.  Source-facing wrappers must be audited against the hypotheses of the particular Section~2, 6, 7, or 8 statement rather than cited interchangeably.
\item Direct rotation extremality is conceptually central but can remain a separate module from the inequalities themselves.
\end{enumerate}
