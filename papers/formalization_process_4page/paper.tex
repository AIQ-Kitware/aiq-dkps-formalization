\begin{abstract}
Recent LLM and agentic tooling has lowered the practical barrier to producing
formal mathematics in Lean.  This creates an approachable entry point for
researchers who do not begin as Lean experts, but it also creates a second task:
determining whether a checked formal statement expresses the mathematics that
was intended.  We report lessons from one such experience, an LLM-assisted
formalization of the 1970 Davis--Kahan paper.  The workflow began with manual
copy/paste between chat and an editor and gradually acquired explicit source
tracking, independent semantic review, and a local dashboard that places source
passages, correspondence notes, and compiler-derived Lean evidence together.
We place these practices in the context of recent systems for semantic review,
including EconCSLib, Lean Atlas, ATLAS/AutoformBot, ShadowBench, LeanMarathon,
and FormaTheoria.  A retrospective Git-history analysis finds
\SemanticReversalCount{} transitions in which a result previously accepted by
semantic review was later reopened, affecting \SemanticReversalResultCount{} of
29 tracked Davis--Kahan results.  These observations come from one evolving
project.  We use them as practical examples; they do not estimate model accuracy.
\end{abstract}

\section{From checked proofs to source correspondence}

Lean checks whether a formal proposition follows from its formal hypotheses
\citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  A source-driven formalization requires an
additional judgment: whether that proposition represents the theorem, scope,
and mathematical objects intended by the source.  A theorem can compile while
using a narrower domain, stronger assumptions, a different norm class, or an
alternate representation whose relation to the source has not been justified.
Recent work studies this gap through semantic critics, formal-equivalence tests,
human review, and project-scale tooling
\citep{peng2025criticlean,zhang2026beyondcompilation,han2026shadowbench}.

Our experience began with a comparatively simple workflow: describe a theorem
to a conversational model, copy generated Lean into an editor, report compiler
errors, and repeat.  Missing spectral and operator-theory foundations frequently
blocked progress, and we did not initially know how to decompose those gaps into
feasible formal tasks.  As later agentic models became capable of constructing
larger prerequisite chains and completing longer proofs, our main uncertainty
shifted toward interpretation.  A proof-producing context could report that a
Davis--Kahan result had been formalized while a fresh review later identified a
finite-dimensional, bounded-operator, norm-class, or representation restriction
absent from the paper.

\section{Practical lessons from the case study}
\label{sec:lessons}

\paragraph{Fix the mathematical target before judging completion.}
``Formalize Davis--Kahan'' was too vague to support a reliable completion claim.
The 1970 paper contains a family of related results with standing conventions,
multiple angle constructions, real and complex settings, bounded and unbounded
operators, and several norm scopes \citep{davis1970rotation}.  We eventually
tracked the paper's stated results explicitly and recorded the source passages
needed to interpret each one.  For a newcomer, the practical lesson is to name
the exact source claim and the scope being claimed before asking whether a
formalization is finished.

\paragraph{Read the elaborated statement, not only the proof status.}
Compilation establishes a theorem about the proposition Lean received.  A
semantic review should therefore inspect the theorem's elaborated type and the
definitions that determine its meaning.  This caught cases where a valid Lean
result lived in a narrower setting than the paper.  Systems such as Lean Atlas
attack a related scaling problem by identifying the project declarations whose
semantics can affect selected target theorems \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.

\paragraph{Treat decomposition as part of formalization.}
When an agent reports that a required foundation is missing, the next useful
step may be to split that missing result into smaller obligations.  Repeated prompting for the
endpoint was less useful.  In our project, progress accelerated once
agentic models could build prerequisite chains around results such as
Courant--Fischer and Weyl.  This changed our role from relaying compiler errors
toward selecting and reviewing intermediate mathematical obligations.

\paragraph{Use review contexts with an explicit falsification objective.}
We repeatedly placed apparently completed results into fresh contexts and asked
for a concrete mismatch with the source.  The review prompts check hypotheses,
conclusions, constants, object identity, scalar field, dimension, operator
domain, norm class, gap assumptions, inherited conventions, and representation
changes.  LeanMarathon and FormaTheoria independently make semantic or
adversarial review explicit parts of longer formalization workflows
\citep{zhang2026leanmarathon,nie2026formatheoria}.  Our history supports this as
a useful practice in this project; it does not establish a general performance
advantage for independent reviewers.

\paragraph{Persist what was reviewed.}
Conversational confidence is difficult to maintain once statements and source
interpretations change.  We therefore began recording the source fragment, the
Lean statement, the semantic relation being asserted, and hashes that flag a
previous review after either side changes.  EconCSLib provides a closely related
author-facing workflow with paper statements, Lean statements, LLM assistance,
human review controls, dependency graphs, and validation reports
\citep{garg2026econcs}.  LeanArchitect addresses maintenance through synchronized
informal/formal blueprint metadata \citep{zhu2026leanarchitect}.  These systems
provide useful starting points for researchers who need this infrastructure.

\section{One implementation: a semantic-review dashboard}
\label{sec:dashboard}

Our local dashboard grew from the preceding practices.  It organizes a reviewed
result into three lanes: the mathematical source, the correspondence record, and
Lean evidence.  The source lane can include the displayed theorem together with
inherited assumptions or definitions needed to interpret it.  The correspondence
lane decomposes the claim into setup, hypotheses, conclusions, scope, and
representation relations.  The Lean lane displays the declaration, its
compiler-elaborated type, and optional statement closure and dependency
information.  Representation changes can name a bridge theorem, providing formal evidence for
the relation described in prose.

The tool also pins reviewed source fragments and hashes elaborated Lean
statements.  When either changes, the previous semantic acceptance requires
inspection again.  Mechanical evidence and mathematical judgment remain
separate: the compiler can establish the exact formal type and the tool can
identify changed evidence, while a reviewer still decides whether the source
and Lean clauses express the same mathematics.

We view this implementation as one concrete realization of practices that are
now appearing across several projects.  EconCSLib offers a paper-oriented review
dashboard \citep{garg2026econcs}; Lean Atlas provides an interactive semantic
review environment and prunes proof-only dependencies \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas};
ATLAS lets users compare textbook statements with Lean formalizations and
inspect dependency graphs, with automated faithfulness reports generated by
AutoformBot \citep{rammal2026scale}; and ShadowBench provides a compiler-grounded
statement-level semantic test through auxiliary shadow statements
\citep{han2026shadowbench}.  Our dashboard has been most useful for recording
fine-grained source context and correspondence decisions in a changing research
formalization.

\section{Retrospective evidence and limitations}
\label{sec:case}

The maintained Davis--Kahan register contains 29 source results.  Twenty-eight
currently have Lean proofs at the scope asserted by the source, while
Proposition~4.4 is false as printed and is represented by a formal refutation.
The mathematical formalization is reserved for a longer paper; here the set of
29 results gives a stable unit for examining review history.

We analyzed Git history through snapshot \texttt{\SemanticSnapshotShort}.  We
count a \emph{review reversal} when a result marked accepted by semantic review
changes to a nonaccepted state at the next revision of the maintained register.
This gives \SemanticReversalCount{} reversals across
\SemanticReversalResultCount{} of the 29 results.  The count includes
mathematical-scope findings, correspondence-record defects, and cases where a
stronger checker invalidated evidence previously treated as sufficient.

These events explain why our review process acquired stricter scope checks,
explicit representation bridges, and drift-sensitive pins.  They do not form
independent trials.  Models, prompts, human knowledge, source interpretation,
and the checker itself all changed during the project.  Git co-author trailers
and saved session identifiers preserve model provenance for some episodes, but
many interactions cannot be reconstructed at that level.  The reversal count
should therefore be read as a historical description of one evolving project.

\section{Conclusion}

Researchers can now reach substantial Lean developments through LLM-assisted
tools before they have accumulated deep experience with Lean or with every part
of the source mathematics.  Our Davis--Kahan effort suggests a practical set of
habits for that situation: identify the exact source claim, inspect the
elaborated formal statement, decompose missing foundations, ask independent
reviewers to search for concrete mismatches, and preserve the evidence behind
accepted correspondence judgments.  Recent systems provide increasingly mature
implementations of many of these ideas.  Our local dashboard records how these
needs appeared in one project and provides a concrete case study for comparing
approaches to semantic review.
