\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) can now produce substantial Lean developments for
researchers who are still learning Lean.  We used these tools to formalize the
29 mathematical results tracked from Davis and Kahan's 1970 paper and present
the project as a case study in semantic alignment: whether checked Lean
statements express the mathematics in their source.  Alongside our experience,
we collect \PractitionerAccountCount{} public first-person accounts of AI-assisted
Lean work, which report very different levels of human Lean inspection.  We
observe that LLMs often reported completion prematurely.  All 29 tracked results
now have checked formal treatments: 28 printed results have Lean proofs, while
Proposition~4.4 is certified false as printed and has a repaired theorem.
Source-level correspondence review remains ongoing.  The structured data,
source notes, and reproduction scripts accompany the paper.
\ifshowartifacturl\space Public materials: \url{\artifacturl}.\fi
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Formalization adds another layer of trust to a mathematical argument: Lean
checks that a proof term establishes the proposition encoded in its theorem
statement \citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  Transferring that guarantee to
a source theorem requires the formal statement to have the intended hypotheses,
objects, and scope.  Lu et al. use \emph{semantic alignment} for this agreement
between an informal mathematical statement and its formalization
\citep{lu2025formalalign}; ShadowBench studies the same problem
\citep{han2026shadowbench}.  Here the intended claim is the mathematics expressed
by Davis and Kahan's 1970 paper together with the definitions, inherited
assumptions, notation, and mathematical context needed to read it.  Timothy
Chow's ``doohickeys'' parable captures the issue: a researcher may intend to
formalize the statement that all doohickeys are excellent, while an agent
formalizes instead that all special doohickeys are quasi-excellent.  The review
question is whether the agent formalized the claim the researcher intended
\citep{chow2026mindreading}.

The distinction appeared in both directions during this project.  Formalizing
Davis and Kahan's Proposition~4.4 exposed that the printed proposition is false;
we obtained a finite-dimensional counterexample and a repaired statement.
Source-to-signature comparison also exposed cases where our checked Lean results
did not yet cover the full source statement.

Effective formalization depends on reusable foundations: later projects can
build on checked definitions and theorems instead of reconstructing their
prerequisites.  Mathlib already provides a broad common base \citep{mathlib2020},
and Tau Ceti is one effort to expand that base with AI-authored Lean
\citep{tauceti2026}.  Davis--Kahan was a useful target because we wanted reusable
spectral-perturbation results for downstream applications, and the 1970 paper
ranges from matrices to Hilbert-space operators \citep{davis1970rotation}.  We
therefore set out to formalize all 29 mathematical results in the paper while
developing reusable operator-theoretic foundations along the way.

Our process became more explicit as source-level discrepancies appeared.
We first fixed the target by recording each counted result, its source location,
and the scope that a faithful formalization had to retain.  For each target,
LLMs decomposed missing mathematics into intermediate obligations, searched
Mathlib, local theory, and the literature for reusable foundations, implemented
Lean, and used compiler feedback to repair proofs.  A successful compile then
entered a separate source-to-signature review, often with a different model and
human inspection of the source.  That review checked scalar field, dimension,
bounded or unbounded operator scope, norm class, gap assumptions, and whether
objects such as residuals and angle operators were the ones used by the paper.
A mismatch sent the result back through review and could trigger another round
of decomposition or foundation work.  The model roles were separated loosely:
Claude Code handled most Lean implementation and compiler-driven repair,
ChatGPT was used heavily for mathematical planning, source comparison, and
review, and Codex participated in both.  A persistent result register kept the
source target, claimed scope, Lean witness, and review state outside the model
conversations.  We later developed \texttt{leanq} to query the pinned Lean
environment for elaborated types, source locations, axioms, and dependencies
when a correspondence question required exact compiler-derived evidence.
\Cref{fig:workflow} summarizes this cycle.

Lean formalization activity is growing rapidly.  Papers With Lean is a curated
index of Lean-related arXiv papers; its public monthly statistics provide one
view of that growth \citep{paperswithlean2026stats}.  The index contains
\LeanPapersTwentyFive{} papers in all of 2025 and already
\LeanPapersJanAugTwentySix{} in January--August 2026, corresponding to a mean
monthly rate about \LeanPublicationRateRatio{} times higher.  \Cref{fig:lean-activity}
reproduces the 2025--2026 series through the last complete month.

This paper studies how researchers supervise source-to-formal-statement
correspondence when LLMs can produce Lean beyond the human's own fluency.  We
compare our experience with public first-person reports and examine a historical
$\sin 2\Theta$ correspondence failure in detail.  The structured account notes
retain the public source, observation, qualification, and fields used for the
descriptive counts.  \Cref{app:data} describes the sources and validation checks.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We asked: when LLMs produce Lean beyond the user's own Lean fluency, what does
the human inspect or delegate before accepting that the formalization matches
the intended mathematics?  Using LLM-assisted web search, we collected
\PractitionerAccountCount{} public first-person accounts from blogs, project
transcripts, social posts, forums, Zulip, and formal case studies.  This
collection is not necessarily representative.  \Cref{app:data} gives the
per-account source records, categorical-field schema, and build-time validation
checks.  Collins et al. separately report a human-subject study of initial
AI-assisted formalization workflows \citep{collins2026workflows}.

\paragraph{How much Lean does the human need to read?}
The accounts do not agree.  Kahle and Saiki describe checking definitions,
hypotheses, and decomposition while agents write much of the proof code
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double}.  Sergey describes stepping in at
representation choices and missing lemmas instead of repairing tactics line by
line \citep{sergey2026move}.  Suomela describes a workflow in which he does not
read the generated Lean before studying the mathematical proof, and Gowers
reports using Aristotle on a complicated paper without needing Lean knowledge
\citep{suomela2026workflow,gowers2026leiden}.  These accounts put the human at
very different distances from the formal code.

Some researchers use another model to mediate the semantic check.  Nowak and
F\"ugger used Claude to review Codex formalizations for added hypotheses,
weakening, and proof holes \citep{nowak2026workflow}; our producer/reviewer split
was similar.  Kova\v{c} asked another model to translate generated Lean back into English
because he could not read it directly \citep{kovac2025erdos189}.  Ilin reports adversarial review among
multiple systems \citep{ilin2026vml}.  Counterexamples often provide a more
human-readable failure signal: Saiki used them to diagnose an underspecified
definition, and Oum corrected a source hypothesis after formalization
\citep{saiki2026double,oum2026preprint}.  Enomoto describes a later ``proof
digestion'' phase in which an already checked argument was reorganized until he
understood it mathematically \citep{enomoto2026essay}.

Within the \PractitionerAccountCount{} accounts, \AccountSemanticMismatchCount{}
explicitly report a statement, definition, scope, or correspondence mismatch,
with \AccountSemanticMismatchQualifiedCount{} additional qualified cases.
\AccountSeparateAIReviewCount{} describe a separate AI review role, and
\AccountPersistentStateCount{} keep substantive project state outside the chat.
Formalization exposed a defect in the informal source in
\AccountSourceDefectCount{} accounts; \AccountCounterexampleCount{} explicitly
used a counterexample to diagnose or communicate a problem.  These counts
describe the public accounts we found; representativeness is unknown.  The
structured rows and their source notes are included with the paper
(\cref{app:data}).

There is also a growing layer of tooling for LLM--Lean interaction.
LeanDojo extracts proof states and premises and supports programmatic Lean
interaction \citep{yang2023leandojo}; Pantograph exposes the Lean frontend,
proof execution, expression construction, and environment inspection
\citep{aniva2024pantograph}; LeanInteract provides a Python interface to Lean's
REPL, including declaration and info-tree extraction \citep{poiroux2025leaninteract}.
For this project we built a smaller query tool, \texttt{leanq}, for asking the
pinned project environment for elaborated statements, declaration kinds,
source locations, axioms, and dependency information.
\ifshowartifacturl\space It is part of
\href{\leanqurl}{\texttt{aiq-lean-formalization-tools}}.\else\space It is included
in the supplementary code.\fi

Other tools focus on semantic alignment itself.  FormalAlign evaluates whether
an informal mathematical statement and its formalization agree in meaning
\citep{lu2025formalalign}.  EconCSLib places source text, Lean statements,
generated mathematical renderings, and human match/mismatch judgments together
\citep{garg2026econcs}.  Lean Atlas and Lean Compass prune proof-only dependencies
to expose a smaller set of declarations whose meanings can affect a target
theorem \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.  ShadowBench probes a formal statement
with auxiliary shadow statements and implication checks
\citep{han2026shadowbench}.

\section{What we formalized}
\label{sec:dk}

Davis and Kahan study how invariant spectral subspaces of a self-adjoint
operator change under perturbation.  In matrix terms, let $A=A^*$ and let $E_0$
have orthonormal columns spanning a candidate subspace: the subspace whose
closeness to an exact invariant spectral subspace we want to measure.  The matrix
$A_0=E_0^*AE_0$ records the action of $A$ after projecting back into the $E_0$
coordinates, while
\[
  R = A E_0 - E_0 A_0
\]
measures the part of $AE_0$ not explained by that within-subspace action.  Under
spectral separation, the paper bounds the principal angles between the candidate
subspace and an exact spectral subspace.  One directed $\sin 2\Theta$ result has
the form
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,N(\sin 2\Theta_0) \le 2N(R),
  \label{eq:sin2theta}
\end{equation}
for a unitarily invariant norm $N$.  The paper carries this picture beyond
finite matrices to real or complex separable Hilbert spaces, including
appropriate unbounded self-adjoint cases \citep{davis1970rotation}.

We set out to formalize the four unnumbered Section~2 headline theorems plus
every named theorem, proposition, lemma, and corollary that Davis and Kahan
establish, for 29 results total.  Definitions, proof steps, examples, immediate
consequences, and open or deferred claims are not counted separately.  We used a
persistent result register with one row per counted result, recording the source
location and scope, the Lean declaration used as evidence, and its review status.
This gave the project a finite checklist for implementation and later source review.

The formalization required foundational layers beyond the final perturbation
inequalities: projection and principal-angle geometry, unitarily invariant norms
and operator ideals, Borel spectral calculus and unbounded self-adjoint operator
theory, real/complex transfer, direct rotations and operator angles, and
Sylvester equations.  These reusable components are being prepared for
contribution to Tau Ceti \citep{tauceti2026}.

Formalization also exposed a problem in the source itself: Proposition~4.4 is
false as printed.  The repository contains a finite-dimensional counterexample
and a repaired theorem \citep{aiqdkps2026repo}.

\subsection{Example of a semantically misaligned formalization}
\label{sec:scope-mismatch}

The directed $\sin 2\Theta$ statement in \cref{eq:sin2theta} uses the
residual $R=AE_0-E_0A_0$ associated with the candidate subspace and includes real and complex, potentially unbounded
self-adjoint settings under the paper's domain and bounded-residual conditions.
At one stage the checked theorem below was used as the ledger witness for this
source result.  It has the printed residual and factor~2, but the signature fixes
the scalar field to $\mathbb{C}$ and represents the ambient operator $A$ as a
bounded \texttt{ContinuousLinearMap}.

\section{Conclusion}

Formalizing the 29 tracked results required more than constructing Lean proofs.
A persistent source-to-Lean result register kept the source location, intended
scope, Lean declaration, and review status visible for each target.  Related
workflows appear in EconCSLib; Lean Atlas/Compass helps expose the declarations
whose meanings can affect a target; and FormaTheoria and LeanArchitect support
longer-running source/formal reconciliation
\citep{garg2026econcs,yanahama2026leanatlas,nie2026formatheoria,zhu2026leanarchitect}.
During this project we also developed \texttt{leanq}, a small query tool for the
pinned Lean environment that returns elaborated theorem types, source locations,
declaration kinds, axioms, and dependencies.  In review, we repeatedly returned
to apparently completed results with a falsification objective and checked the
specific places where source scope can drift: scalar field, dimension, operator
domain, norm class, gap assumptions, and the identity of the residual and angle
objects.

\paragraph{Did we formalize Davis--Kahan?}
All 29 tracked results now have checked formal treatments.  Twenty-eight printed
statements are proved in Lean.  Proposition~4.4 is certified false as printed;
the development gives a finite-dimensional counterexample and proves a repaired
theorem.  The remaining uncertainty is semantic correspondence.  As of this
revision, LLM reviewers are still identifying scope and representation problems
in some formalizations that had previously been marked complete.  We believe the
development is very close, but we are not yet ready to declare every
source-to-Lean correspondence closed.  For this project, completion now requires
both a checked formal result and explicit review that the formal statement
matches the source claim at its intended scope.
