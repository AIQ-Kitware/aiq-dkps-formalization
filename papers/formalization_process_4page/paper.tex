\begin{abstract}
Coding agents can now produce substantial Lean developments for researchers who
are still learning to read Lean.  We ran into the resulting supervision problem
while trying to formalize all 29 mathematical results in Davis and Kahan's 1970
paper.  We put our experience beside \PractitionerAccountCount{} public
first-person accounts of AI-assisted Lean work, where people report very
different answers to a basic question: how much Lean does the human need to
read?  We show one historical false finish from our repository: a checked
$\sin 2\Theta$ inequality with the right residual and constant, but only for
bounded complex operators, while the source also covers real and unbounded
settings.  Our current checks cover all 29 tracked results at the scope we presently
understand the source to require; Proposition~4.4 is false as printed and is
formally refuted and repaired.  The structured data, source notes,
paper-revision prompts, and reproduction scripts accompany the paper.
\ifshowartifacturl\space Public materials: \url{\artifacturl}.\fi
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A Lean proof checker answers a precise question: does this proof inhabit this
formal proposition? \citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}  The difficult question
for a source-driven formalization can come one step earlier: is that proposition
the mathematical claim we intended?  Timothy Chow gives the problem an
intentionally silly formulation.  If the intended theorem says that all
``doohickeys'' are excellent, while the formal statement only says that special
doohickeys are quasi-excellent, a checked proof certifies the weaker statement
\citep{chow2026mindreading}.  The kernel has done its job.

This question has become practical very quickly.  Papers With Lean indexed 174
Lean-related arXiv papers during 2025 and 410 during January--July 2026; July
alone contained 101 \citep{paperswithlean2026stats}.  The mean monthly rate over
those first seven months of 2026 was about \LeanPublicationRateRatio{} times the
2025 rate.  \Cref{fig:lean-activity} in the appendix plots the monthly counts and
marks 13 July 2026, when the dedicated \DK{} development in our Git history
began.

We started with a broad request to formalize Davis and Kahan's paper
\citep{davis1970rotation}.  At first we knew the source mathematics better than
Lean, and we did not yet know the paper well enough to specify what ``all of
Davis--Kahan'' meant.  As the agents improved, proof production stopped being
the main bottleneck.  We could reach checked endpoints faster than we could
build confidence that the endpoints had the right hypotheses, objects, scalar
field, dimension, and bounded or unbounded scope.

This paper records that experience and a snapshot of public reports from other
researchers.  We use one concrete historical mismatch to keep the discussion
anchored to something that actually happened.  The structured notes behind the
public-account snapshot record a source URL, citation key, observation, and the
fields used for the descriptive counts; the Git events used here are kept in a
separate dated table keyed by commit.  \Cref{app:data} describes the files and
their hashes.

\section{How people are supervising AI-assisted Lean}
\label{sec:related}

We read \PractitionerAccountCount{} public first-person accounts from blogs,
project transcripts, social posts, forums, Zulip, and formal case studies.  We
found them by web search and citation chasing; representativeness is unknown.
Collins et al. separately report a human-subject study of initial AI-assisted
formalization workflows \citep{collins2026workflows}.

\paragraph{How much Lean does the human need to read?}
The accounts do not agree.  Kahle and Saiki describe checking definitions,
hypotheses, and decomposition while agents write much of the proof code
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double}.  Sergey describes stepping in at
representation choices and missing lemmas instead of repairing tactics line by
line \citep{sergey2026move}.  Suomela describes a workflow in which he does not
read the generated Lean before studying the mathematical proof, and Gowers
reports using Aristotle on a complicated paper without needing Lean knowledge
\citep{suomela2026workflow,gowers2026leiden}.  These accounts put the human at
very different distances from the formal code.

Some researchers use another model to mediate the semantic check.  Nowak and
F\"ugger used Claude to review Codex formalizations for added hypotheses,
weakening, and proof holes \citep{nowak2026workflow}.  Kova\v{c} asked another
model to translate generated Lean back into English because he could not read
it directly \citep{kovac2025erdos189}.  Ilin reports adversarial review among
multiple systems \citep{ilin2026vml}.  Counterexamples often provide a more
human-readable failure signal: Saiki used them to diagnose an underspecified
definition, and Oum corrected a source hypothesis after formalization
\citep{saiki2026double,oum2026preprint}.  Enomoto describes a later ``proof
digestion'' phase in which an already checked argument was reorganized until he
understood it mathematically \citep{enomoto2026essay}.

Within the \PractitionerAccountCount{} accounts, \AccountSemanticMismatchCount{}
explicitly report a statement, definition, scope, or correspondence mismatch,
with \AccountSemanticMismatchQualifiedCount{} additional qualified cases.
\AccountSeparateAIReviewCount{} describe a separate AI review role, and
\AccountPersistentStateCount{} keep substantive project state outside the chat.
Formalization exposed a defect in the informal source in
\AccountSourceDefectCount{} accounts; \AccountCounterexampleCount{} explicitly
used a counterexample to diagnose or communicate a problem.  These counts
describe the public accounts we found; representativeness is unknown.  The
structured rows and their source notes are included with the paper
(\cref{app:data}).

There is also a growing layer of tooling for the agent--Lean connection.
LeanDojo extracts proof states and premises and supports programmatic Lean
interaction \citep{yang2023leandojo}; Pantograph exposes the Lean frontend,
proof execution, expression construction, and environment inspection
\citep{aniva2024pantograph}; LeanInteract provides a Python interface to Lean's
REPL, including declaration and info-tree extraction \citep{poiroux2025leaninteract}.
For this project we built a smaller query tool, \texttt{leanq}, for asking the
pinned project environment for elaborated statements, declaration kinds,
source locations, axioms, and dependency information.
\ifshowartifacturl\space It is part of
\href{\leanqurl}{\texttt{aiq-lean-formalization-tools}}.\else\space It is included
in the supplementary code.\fi

Other tools focus on the correspondence problem itself.  EconCSLib places
source text, Lean statements, generated mathematical renderings, and human
match/mismatch judgments together \citep{garg2026econcs}.  Lean Atlas and Lean
Compass prune proof-only dependencies to expose a smaller set of declarations
whose meanings can affect a target theorem \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.
ShadowBench probes a formal statement with auxiliary shadow statements and
implication checks \citep{han2026shadowbench}.  These systems are especially
interesting for users who cannot audit a large generated development line by
line.

\section{The Davis--Kahan case}
\label{sec:dk}

\paragraph{What is the theorem about?}
At the matrix level, imagine a symmetric matrix whose eigenvalues contain a
cluster separated from the rest by a gap $\delta$.  A trial subspace is nearly
invariant when the residual
\[
  R = A E_0 - E_0 A_0
\]
is small: applying $A$ to a vector represented by $E_0$ almost agrees with
staying inside the trial coordinates and applying $A_0$.  Davis--Kahan bounds
how far that trial subspace can tilt away from the exact spectral subspace.
The operator $\sin\Theta_0$ measures that angular error, and one headline bound
has the form $\delta\,N(\sin\Theta_0)\le N(R)$ for a unitarily invariant norm
$N$.  The related directed $\sin 2\Theta$ theorem doubles the angle and gives
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,N(\sin(2\Theta_0)) \le 2N(R).
  \label{eq:sin2theta}
\end{equation}
The paper states these results beyond finite matrices: real or complex
separable Hilbert spaces, infinite dimension, and appropriate unbounded
self-adjoint cases are part of the source scope \citep{davis1970rotation}.

The 1970 paper contains four headline angle theorems and 25 additional named
results that we track as 29 mathematical results.  \Cref{fig:workflow} shows the
workflow that emerged while trying to cover them.  Source tracking, separate
review, and reusable foundation work were added as the project grew.  Some
paper-independent operator theory is being prepared for possible contribution
to Tau Ceti \citep{tauceti2026}.

The formalization also exposed a problem in the source itself.  Davis and
Kahan's Proposition~4.4 is false as printed.  The repository contains a
finite-dimensional counterexample and a repaired statement; the longer
formalization manuscript develops that result in detail \citep{aiqdkps2026repo}.

\subsection{A checked theorem that was still too narrow}
\label{sec:false-finish}

The source statement behind \cref{eq:sin2theta} uses the trial residual
$R=AE_0-E_0A_0$ and applies to every unitarily invariant norm.  A historical
Lean theorem in our repository had the right residual, factor~2, and norm
family.  Its signature looked like this:

\begin{lstlisting}[style=leanpaper,
  caption={Historical Lean signature for the directed $\sin 2\Theta$ result.
  The proof compiled.  Surrounding Lean context is omitted.},
  label={lst:false-finish}]
variable {E : Type v}
  [NormedAddCommGroup E] [InnerProductSpace ℂ E] [CompleteSpace E]

theorem sinTwoTheta_directedResidual_paperUINorm
    (N : PaperUnitaryInvariantNorm)
    {A : E →L[ℂ] E} (hA : IsSelfAdjoint A)
    {U V : Submodule ℂ E}
    [U.HasOrthogonalProjection] [V.HasOrthogonalProjection]
    (hU : Reduces A U)
    {a b d : ℝ} (hd : 0 < d) (hab : a ≤ b)
    (hUspec : spectrum ℝ (compressOperator U A) ⊆ Set.Icc a b)
    (hUspec' : ∀ x ∈ spectrum ℝ (compressOperator Uᗮ A),
      x ≤ a - d ∨ b + d ≤ x)
    (M : V →L[ℂ] V)
    (hMem : N.Mem (residual A V.subtypeL M)) :
    N.Mem (sinTwoThetaIdealBlock U V) ∧
      d * N.gauge (sinTwoThetaIdealBlock U V) ≤
        2 * N.gauge (residual A V.subtypeL M) := by
  -- proof omitted
\end{lstlisting}

The restriction is visible near the top: the ambient space is complex and
$A:E\to_L[\mathbb C]E$ is bounded.  The source also covers the real case and
unbounded self-adjoint operators under its domain and bounded-residual
conditions.  At the same time we had an unbounded $\sin 2\Theta$ theorem whose
right-hand side used a reflection residual, a different operator from the
printed trial residual.  A source-to-Lean comparison on 12 August 2026 identified the gap: one checked
theorem had the right residual with incomplete scope, while another carried the
unbounded scope with the wrong residual for this source statement.  The repair
constructed the reflection argument internally and exposed real and complex
unbounded endpoints with the source's trial residual.

The Lean theorem above was valid.  The overclaim was the correspondence from the
paper to that theorem.  This is close to Chow's doohickey parable, except that
the difference was hidden in scalar field, boundedness, and the identity of the
residual rather than in deliberately silly names.

\section{Discussion}

Our current review page places the source passage, inherited source context,
correspondence notes, and compiler-derived Lean statement together.  It is
backed by \texttt{leanq} queries against the pinned environment and by hashes of
the source and statement used for a review.  \Cref{fig:dashboard} shows the
sine-theta page in the appendix.  The interface still asks a lot from a reader
who barely knows Lean: it exposes the theorem more readily than the meanings of
the structures, predicates, coercions, and typeclasses appearing inside it.  A
useful next step is theorem-centered definition inspection, using the exact
project and Mathlib revisions, together with a Compass-like reduction to the
semantic vocabulary that can affect the statement.

\paragraph{How much Lean did we actually read?}
At the start of this project, the first author could not reliably audit a Lean
theorem signature.  By the time of this paper, we could recognize many of the
scope decisions that had caused trouble---real versus complex scalars,
finite-dimensional assumptions, bounded maps, and the shape of a residual---and
follow definitions when something looked suspicious.  We still used LLMs and
small query tools to explain unfamiliar structures, walk through bundled
predicates, and compare a Lean statement with the source.  The historical
\(\sin 2\Theta\) example in \cref{lst:false-finish} is easy to diagnose once
one knows that \texttt{\ensuremath{\mathbb C}} fixes the scalar field and
\texttt{ContinuousLinearMap} gives a bounded operator.  Other scope choices can
sit several definitions away from the displayed theorem.  That gap between
``able to produce Lean'' and ``able to read every semantic dependency'' is the
reason we built the review page in the first place.

\paragraph{What does 29/29 mean here?}
The denominator comes directly from the paper: four unnumbered theorems in
Section~2 plus every named theorem, proposition, lemma, and corollary, for 29
tracked results.  For each one, the repository records the source location and
the Lean declaration used as evidence.  The tooling checks that the declaration
still exists and compiles, and it hashes the source passage and Lean statement
that were reviewed so later edits are visible.  The source-to-Lean
correspondence itself remains a review judgment.  At the current snapshot, the
project has no known unresolved mismatch in those 29 records: 28 printed
results have checked proofs, and Proposition~4.4 has a checked counterexample
and repaired theorem.  So 29/29 is our current coverage count.  It carries
no calibrated probability that another review will fail to find a mistake.

\paragraph{Can repeated review buy statistical confidence?}
Repeated attempts to find a correspondence defect should change our confidence,
but our historical reviews were not standardized.  A simple model makes the
missing measurement explicit.  If discoveries followed a Poisson process with
rate $\lambda$ per unit of comparable review exposure, then the probability of
no discovery during an additional exposure $h$ would be
\[
  \Pr(\text{no new defect in }h\mid\lambda)=e^{-\lambda h}.
\]
Our old logs record dates, commits, and findings; they do not provide comparable
reviewer-hours or fixed review passes, and the models, prompts, tooling, and our
own understanding changed.  We therefore do not estimate $\lambda$.  The
Bayesian version of the stopping calculation is given in \cref{app:stopping}.
A future study could record fixed review passes or reviewer-hours from the
start.

As of the snapshot used for this paper, our tooling associates all 29 tracked
\DK{} results with checked Lean evidence at the scope we currently believe the
source requires.  Twenty-eight are proved as printed and Proposition~4.4 is
refuted and repaired.  Did we really formalize Davis--Kahan?  And the honest
answer at this point is: I don't know, probably. If not, it's pretty close.

The drafting of this paper also used an LLM.  The supplementary prompt log keeps
the human inputs verbatim, including corrections that rejected earlier
LLM-generated framings about what a novice Lean user would realistically do.
The same directory contains the account snapshot, publication counts, Git
timeline, resource snapshot, and material hashes.  Those records are the basis
for the factual claims above.
