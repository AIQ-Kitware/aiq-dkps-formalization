\begin{abstract}
Lean formalization changed quickly during 2026.  Papers With Lean indexed
\LeanPapersTwentyFive{} Lean-related arXiv papers across 2025 and
\LeanPapersJanJulTwentySix{} from January through July 2026, about a
\LeanPublicationRateRatio-fold increase in average monthly rate.  Researchers
also began publishing first-person accounts of using coding agents and theorem
provers with very different levels of Lean fluency.  We read
\PractitionerAccountCount{} such accounts and recorded concrete details about
human roles, AI-mediated review, statement mismatches, counterexamples, and
later efforts to understand checked proofs.  \AccountSemanticMismatchCount{}
accounts explicitly report a mismatch between a formal statement, definition,
or claimed correspondence and the intended target; \AccountSourceDefectCount{}
report formalization exposing a defect in the informal source.  We compare
these experiences with our Davis--Kahan project, begun in July 2026, where fresh
adversarial review repeatedly reopened correspondence judgments that had
already been accepted and eventually motivated a semantic-review interface.
The recurring problem is supervision across a language boundary: the human may
understand the mathematics while depending on AI to produce, explain, and
challenge Lean.  The source notes and data behind the counts in this paper are
public.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A Lean kernel checks a formal proposition against a formal proof
\citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  Researchers who start from ordinary
mathematics also have to decide whether the formal proposition captures the
result they care about.  Agentic coding systems have widened the set of people
who can reach that point.  A mathematician can now hand a paper to an agent,
let it search Mathlib, build missing infrastructure, repair compiler errors, and
produce thousands of lines of checked Lean while learning the formal language
along the way.

The scale of public activity changed sharply over the period covered by this
paper.  The Papers With Lean index records \LeanPapersTwentyFive{} Lean-related
arXiv papers during 2025 and \LeanPapersJanJulTwentySix{} during the first seven
months of 2026; July alone contains \LeanPapersJulyTwentySix{}
\citep{paperswithlean2026stats}.  The average monthly rate for January--July
2026 is about \LeanPublicationRateRatio{} times the 2025 rate.  This is a rough
measure of Lean research activity, and it captures the timing of the change we
experienced.  The dedicated Davis--Kahan portion of our repository begins on
13 July 2026, near the steep end of this rise.

Public reports from the same period describe a new practical situation.  Saiki
entered an autoformalization project with very little proof-assistant experience
and later found extra hypotheses inserted by the agent
\citep{saiki2026double}.  Nowak began with no experience reading Lean and used a
second agent to challenge the first agent's translation
\citep{nowak2026workflow}.  Kova\v{c}, also new to Lean, used model
back-translation to inspect an Aristotle-produced theorem
\citep{kovac2025erdos189}.  Suomela describes letting the formalization reach a
checked endpoint before reading the generated Lean, then turning to the
mathematical proof \citep{suomela2026workflow}.  The human role differs across
these accounts, yet each person still has to decide what confidence in the
artifact means.

Our own workflow changed for the same reason.  It began with manual copy/paste
between a chatbot and an editor.  Later agents worked directly inside the Lean
environment and could descend into missing foundations.  We then spent more
attention on the relation between the source mathematics and the checked
endpoint.  Fresh review contexts, source-result tracking, and the interface in
\cref{fig:semantic-dashboard} grew out of that shift.  \Cref{fig:workflow}
shows the workflow that had emerged by the end of the project.

\section{What people report}
\label{sec:accounts}

We assembled a snapshot of \PractitionerAccountCount{} public first-person
accounts spanning blogs, project transcripts, social posts, forum discussions,
Zulip, and formal case studies
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double,ilin2026vml,miller2026vlasov,
armstrong2026homogenization,tao2026sendov,nowak2026workflow,suomela2026workflow,
kovac2025erdos189,enomoto2026essay,oum2026preprint,hirai2026fri,robertj2026ac,
ennis2026experience,gowers2026leiden,chow2026aristotle,issai2026understanding,
davis2026partial,chafai2026leanai,alexeev2025erdos}.  Search and citation chasing
produced the source list.  For each account we recorded a small set of facts the
source states explicitly: reported Lean experience, whether the author read
generated Lean, use of separate AI review, statement or definition mismatches,
counterexamples, source corrections, persistent project state, and later human
understanding.  Unreported fields stay unreported.  The supplementary source
notes contain the URL and evidence note for every entry.

Collins et al. provide a useful point of comparison through a survey and a
seven-person user study conducted in 2025 \citep{collins2026workflows}.  Their
participants generally wanted high-level human control and six of seven used
more than one AI tool.  Every participant in the user study had at least basic
Lean experience.  The public accounts include several people who explicitly
say they had little or no Lean fluency when the described work began.

\paragraph{People supervise at different levels.}
Kahle, Saiki, Armstrong and Kuusi, Hirai, and others describe reading formal
definitions or theorem statements while agents write much of the proof code
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double,armstrong2026homogenization,
hirai2026fri}.  Their interventions concern definitions, missing assumptions,
mathematical decomposition, and library gaps.  Sergey gives a similar account
from an experienced formal-methods perspective: the useful human intervention
was recognizing a missing weakening lemma, choosing a representation, or
stopping an agent that had spent hours on a bad direction
\citep{sergey2026move}.

Other accounts move some of the reading through another model.  Nowak and
F\"ugger used Claude to check Codex's claimed formalizations for extra
hypotheses, weakening, and remaining proof holes \citep{nowak2026workflow}.
Kova\v{c} stripped contextual clues from generated Lean and asked Gemini to
translate the theorem back into English \citep{kovac2025erdos189}.  Ilin used
multiple specialized systems and adversarial review \citep{ilin2026vml}.
Hirai alternated between Claude-generated decompositions and Aristotle, which
sometimes returned counterexamples to Claude's proposed lemmas
\citep{hirai2026fri}.  \AccountSeparateAIReviewCount{} accounts in the snapshot
explicitly describe a separate AI review role.

Suomela gives the clearest endpoint-first example: Lean can run as a checking
backend while the human postpones attention to the generated formal code
\citep{suomela2026workflow}.  Gowers likewise reports using Aristotle on a
complicated paper without needing Lean knowledge \citep{gowers2026leiden}.
Enomoto describes a later phase of ``proof digestion,'' asking questions about
an already checked argument until it had a mathematical interpretation he
understood \citep{enomoto2026essay}.  These accounts put human understanding at
different points in the process.

\paragraph{The failures are often about the target itself.}
\AccountSemanticMismatchCount{} of the \PractitionerAccountCount{} accounts
explicitly describe a formal statement, definition, or claimed correspondence
that differed from the intended target.  Saiki and Nowak report extra
hypotheses \citep{saiki2026double,nowak2026workflow}.  Armstrong and Kuusi track
Lean statements whose scope differs from the source theorem
\citep{armstrong2026homogenization}.  Alexeev records examples involving wrong
variables, flipped inequalities or quantifiers, and missing hypotheses across
Erd\H{o}s formalizations \citep{alexeev2025erdos}.  Chow reports an agent
returning a file after deleting the target theorem and definitions
\citep{chow2026aristotle}.

The formalization process can also change the informal mathematics.
\AccountSourceDefectCount{} accounts report a source defect found during the
work.  Saiki's counterexamples exposed an underspecified rounding definition
and a missing restriction \citep{saiki2026double}; Oum corrected a missing
non-negativity hypothesis after an Aristotle formalization
\citep{oum2026preprint}.  Enomoto reports a false proof-essential manuscript
claim found during Lean formalization, followed by a counterexample and a new
proof \citep{enomoto2026essay}.  A counterexample or changed hypothesis gives
the human something compact to inspect even when the surrounding Lean
development is large.

Recent systems are beginning to build interfaces around the same pressure.
EconCSLib places paper text, Lean statements, generated renderings, and human
match/mismatch judgments together \citep{garg2026econcs}.  Lean Atlas narrows
the declarations whose semantics require attention \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.
ShadowBench uses auxiliary formal statements and implication checks to test
semantic alignment \citep{han2026shadowbench}.  LeanMarathon and FormaTheoria
carry target review and provenance through longer agentic projects
\citep{zhang2026leanmarathon,nie2026formatheoria}.  The practitioner accounts
show why these systems are arriving now: proof production has moved quickly,
and human supervision is being improvised in parallel.

\section{Davis--Kahan: a supervision problem in one project}
\label{sec:dk}

We began the Davis--Kahan work with limited Lean fluency and a broad instruction
to formalize Davis and Kahan's 1970 paper \citep{davis1970rotation}.  Early work
was dominated by compilation errors and missing foundations.  As the agents
improved, they could build prerequisite chains for spectral and operator theory.
The difficult part shifted to deciding whether an endpoint matched the source.
A producing agent would report completion; a fresh session asked to find a
mismatch would identify a finite-dimensional specialization, a bounded-operator
result where the source used unbounded self-adjoint operators, a narrower norm
class, or a missing representation bridge.

Part of the difficulty came from the target itself.  ``Davis--Kahan in full
generality'' was too vague for us at the beginning because we did not yet
understand the paper well enough to enumerate its theorem family and standing
assumptions.  Studying the mathematics changed the questions we could ask the
agents.  The project eventually kept a 29-result register tied to source
passages and separate semantic-review state.  Across the Git history,
\SemanticReversalCount{} accepted semantic judgments were later reopened,
affecting \SemanticReversalResultCount{} of the 29 results.  Each reversal
records a correspondence judgment that needed more work on scope,
representation, mapping, or review evidence.

The reversals were often concrete.  On 12 August, a fresh review reopened three
counted results because the selected Lean declarations did not actually state
the printed source results; the register moved from 28 of 29 terminal results
to 25 of 29 until those mappings were repaired.  On 31 August, another full
review found four semantic or registration defects together with four defects
in the checker itself.  On 2 September, a hostile review reopened a
correspondence judgment about half an hour after the register had again reached
29 of 29.  We did not plan this review cadence in advance.  It emerged because
fresh contexts kept finding concrete questions after the producing context had
stopped doing so.

The interface in \cref{fig:semantic-dashboard} grew out of these reviews.  The
left lane contains source text and inherited mathematical context.  The middle
lane records explicit claims about how setup, hypotheses, scope, and conclusion
map into the formalization.  One model can produce those claims and another can
attack them.  The right lane anchors the discussion to the compiler-derived
Lean declaration.  A user can ask an LLM to explain or challenge that
declaration in mathematical language; a Lean expert can inspect the same
evidence directly.

For this theorem, inherited source context carries the scope to Hilbert spaces
and unbounded self-adjoint operators, while the Lean endpoint uses project
abstractions for the angle operator and symmetric norming.  Earlier reviews had
lost exactly this kind of scope while looking at endpoints in isolation.  The
interface keeps source text, correspondence claims, and compiler evidence in
one place, and hashes mark a previous review stale when the source or reviewed
statement changes.

\section{Discussion}

The public accounts make model-mediated supervision visible as a real user
practice.  Some researchers read generated definitions and theorem statements.
Some ask another model to translate or challenge them.  Some let the prover run
ahead and work on understanding after a checked endpoint exists.  The common
problem is that mathematical expertise and Lean fluency can be far apart, while
the human remains responsible for deciding whether the result is the one they
care about.

Several artifacts recur because they compress the review problem.  A
counterexample can expose a bad definition without requiring a human to read an
entire development.  A source-to-statement record can preserve which scope was
reviewed.  Persistent plans, TODO files, Git history, correspondence notes, and
prompt provenance appear in \AccountPersistentStateCount{} of the collected
accounts \citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double,ilin2026vml,tao2026sendov,
davis2026partial}.  Enomoto's proof digestion suggests another interface goal:
help a mathematician understand an already verified result in ordinary
mathematical terms \citep{enomoto2026essay}.

The snapshot comes from public, self-selected accounts found through search and
citation chasing.  Projects differ sharply in domain, scale, author expertise,
and tool capability.  The counts establish that these experiences have been
reported by multiple practitioners.  Population frequency and model comparisons
remain open empirical questions.  The Davis--Kahan history adds one detailed
trajectory through this period, with changing models, prompts, mathematical
understanding, and review tooling.

The practical question for the next generation of tools is therefore concrete:
how can a mathematician interrogate a formal artifact through the mathematics
they understand, while retaining enough connection to the checked Lean object
for another human or model to challenge the translation?  The practitioner
accounts, the emerging review systems, and our own project all point to this as
a growing part of AI-assisted formalization.
