\begin{abstract}
We report an LLM-assisted Lean formalization of the 29 mathematical results
tracked from Davis and Kahan's 1970 paper.  The project is a case study in
semantic alignment: whether machine-checked formal statements preserve the
mathematical claims made in the source.  Lean verifies proofs of encoded
propositions, while source correspondence still requires checking hypotheses,
objects, and scope.  The current development contains checked treatments for
all 29 tracked results, including a counterexample and repaired theorem for
Proposition~4.4.  We describe the formalization and source-review process,
examples of discrepancies found during development, and
\PractitionerAccountCount{} public first-person accounts of AI-assisted Lean
work.  The case study illustrates why successful compilation alone does not
establish that an existing theorem has been formalized as intended.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Lean checks that a proof term establishes the proposition encoded in a theorem
statement \citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  Establishing that the proposition
expresses a source theorem also requires checking its hypotheses, objects, and
scope.  This agreement is called \emph{semantic alignment}
\citep{lu2025formalalign}.  For a research paper, the intended claim includes
definitions, inherited assumptions, and surrounding mathematical context.
Chow illustrates the risk with an agent that replaces ``all doohickeys are
excellent'' by ``all special doohickeys are quasi-excellent''
\citep{chow2026mindreading}.  A valid proof of the replacement would leave the
intended theorem unresolved.

We encountered this problem while formalizing Davis and Kahan's 1970
spectral-perturbation results \citep{davis1970rotation}.  We wanted reusable
bounds for downstream applications, including the paper's real and complex
Hilbert-space settings.  Source review exposed both formal statements that
covered less than the source and a false printed proposition.  This paper
examines how we organized that review when LLMs could produce more Lean than
we could inspect line by line.

The process in \cref{fig:workflow} developed as discrepancies appeared.  We
recorded each source claim and its scope, decomposed missing mathematics,
searched Mathlib and the literature for foundations, and used compiler feedback
to repair Lean implementations.  After compilation, a skeptical review compared
the source with the theorem signature and the definitions it used.  Reviewers
checked scalar field, dimension, operator domain, norm class, gap assumptions,
and the identity of residuals and angle operators.  A mismatch could require a
new statement, a different representation, or further foundational work.

The review concerned theorem statements in their mathematical context.  A Lean
proof could use a different argument from Davis and Kahan's.  Reviewers instead
needed to determine what each assumption allowed and what the conclusion
established.  When the formalization represented an angle or residual by another
operator, the correspondence required a mathematical identification of those
objects.  Inspecting a theorem name and its final inequality could miss a
restriction in a type or an inherited hypothesis.  Repeated review also exposed
a risk in the review loop itself: criticism can enlarge the target by asking for
a particular proof route or by choosing a formal meaning for source prose that
does not specify one.  We therefore constrain source-fidelity review to public
theorem statements and the mathematical source context needed to interpret
them.

Claude Code handled most implementation and compiler-driven repair; ChatGPT
was used heavily for planning and source review; Codex participated in both.
A persistent result register kept one row per source result, recording its
location and scope, the Lean declaration used as evidence, and the review
state.  We developed \texttt{leanq} to inspect compiler-derived declarations,
elaborated types, source locations, axioms, and dependencies in the project's
pinned Lean environment.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Using LLM-assisted web search, we collected \PractitionerAccountCount{} public
first-person accounts of AI-assisted Lean work.  They describe different ways
of deciding whether generated Lean expresses the intended mathematics.
The collection is not necessarily representative; Appendix~\ref{app:data}
describes the accompanying source records and qualifications.  Collins
et al. separately study initial human--AI formalization workflows
\citep{collins2026workflows}.

Kahle and Saiki describe checking definitions, hypotheses, and decomposition
while agents write much of the proof code
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double}.  Sergey intervenes at representation
choices and missing lemmas \citep{sergey2026move}.  By contrast, Suomela describes
studying the mathematical proof without first reading the generated Lean, and
Gowers reports using Aristotle on a complicated paper without needing Lean
knowledge \citep{suomela2026workflow,gowers2026leiden}.  Enomoto describes a later
``proof digestion'' phase, reorganizing a checked argument until he understood
it mathematically \citep{enomoto2026essay}.

Other accounts use a second model to mediate review.  Nowak and F\"ugger used
Claude to check Codex formalizations for added hypotheses, weakened statements,
and proof holes \citep{nowak2026workflow}.  Kova\v{c} asked another model to
translate Lean into English because he could not read it directly
\citep{kovac2025erdos189}; Ilin reports adversarial review among multiple systems
\citep{ilin2026vml}.  Counterexamples can expose errors in the intended
mathematics: Saiki diagnosed an underspecified definition, and Oum corrected a
source hypothesis after formalization \citep{saiki2026double,oum2026preprint}.

Within the \PractitionerAccountCount{} accounts, \AccountSemanticMismatchCount{}
explicitly report a statement, definition, scope, or correspondence mismatch,
with \AccountSemanticMismatchQualifiedCount{} additional qualified cases.
\AccountSeparateAIReviewCount{} describe a separate AI review role, and
\AccountPersistentStateCount{} keep substantive project state outside the chat.
Formalization exposed a source defect in \AccountSourceDefectCount{} accounts;
\AccountCounterexampleCount{} explicitly used a counterexample to diagnose or
communicate a problem.  These categories can overlap and describe only the
accounts collected here.

Semantic-review tools address several parts of this process.  FormalAlign
evaluates informal/formal agreement \citep{lu2025formalalign}; ShadowBench tests
formal statements through auxiliary statements and implication checks
\citep{han2026shadowbench}.  EconCSLib presents source text, Lean statements,
mathematical renderings, and human match/mismatch judgments together
\citep{garg2026econcs}.  Lean Atlas and Lean Compass prune proof-only dependencies
to expose definitions that can affect a theorem's meaning
\citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.  LeanMarathon uses adversarial target review,
FormaTheoria supports independent review and source reconciliation, and
LeanArchitect synchronizes informal blueprint metadata with Lean declarations
\citep{zhang2026leanmarathon,nie2026formatheoria,zhu2026leanarchitect}.
These systems provide context for our result register and review interface.

Compiler access is supported by LeanDojo, Pantograph, and LeanInteract
\citep{yang2023leandojo,aniva2024pantograph,poiroux2025leaninteract}.
Our \texttt{leanq} tool provides focused queries for inspecting statements and
their context; it is included in the supplementary code.

\section{What we formalized}
\label{sec:dk}

Davis and Kahan bound the change in invariant subspaces under self-adjoint
perturbations.  In matrix terms, let $A=A^*$ and let $E_0$ have orthonormal
columns spanning a \emph{candidate subspace}, which is to be compared with an
exact invariant subspace.  The compression $A_0=E_0^*AE_0$ records the action of
$A$ after projecting back into the $E_0$ coordinates.  The residual
\[
  R=AE_0-E_0A_0
\]
measures failure of invariance: it vanishes exactly when $A$ preserves the
candidate subspace.  Principal angles measure the angles between corresponding
subspace directions.  With spectral separation $\delta>0$, one directed
$\sin 2\Theta$ result has the form
\begin{equation}
  \delta\,N(\sin 2\Theta_0) \le 2N(R),
  \label{eq:sin2theta}
\end{equation}
where $N$ is a unitarily invariant norm and $\Theta_0$ denotes the directed
angle from the candidate to the exact subspace.  Here the gap separates the
spectrum on the exact subspace from that on its orthogonal complement.
The source also covers real and complex separable Hilbert spaces, including
unbounded self-adjoint operators under its domain and bounded-residual
conditions \citep{davis1970rotation}.  Appendix~\ref{app:formalization1} gives a
reader's guide to the mathematics and the Lean signature below.

Our 29 targets comprise the four unnumbered headline theorems in Section~2 and
every named theorem, proposition, lemma, and corollary established in the paper.
Appendix~\ref{app:data} specifies the counting convention.  At the manuscript
snapshot, the latest source-to-signature review reported no remaining material
mismatch.  We have not yet run a further independent review after that pass, so
we treat this as the current review state rather than a guarantee of complete
semantic correspondence.

The development required projection and principal-angle geometry,
singular-subspace geometry, unitarily invariant norms and operator ideals,
Borel spectral calculus, unbounded self-adjoint operator theory, real/complex
transfer, direct rotations, and Sylvester equations.  These reusable components
are being prepared for contribution to Tau Ceti \citep{tauceti2026}.

\subsection{A historical directed \texorpdfstring{$\sin 2\Theta$}{sin 2 Theta} mismatch}
\label{sec:scope-mismatch}

At one stage, Formalization~\ref{lst:sin2theta-mismatch} was the checked
declaration recorded for \cref{eq:sin2theta}.  It has the printed residual and
factor~2, but fixes the scalar field to $\mathbb C$ and takes $A$ to be a bounded
\texttt{ContinuousLinearMap}.  In the signature, $U$ is the exact reducing
subspace, $V$ the candidate, and \texttt{M} an operator on $V$; choosing the
compression recovers $A_0$ above.

The spectral hypotheses place the spectrum on $U$ in $[a,b]$ and that on
$U^\perp$ outside $(a-\delta,b+\delta)$.  The norm parameter determines an
operator ideal; \texttt{hMem} requires the residual to belong to it.  The
conclusion gives both ideal membership for the directed doubled-angle block
and the norm bound.  These clauses supply the mathematical content of the
bounded result; they do not extend the scalar field or the operator domain.

A separate checked theorem covered unbounded operators but used a reflection
residual, a different operator from $R$.  Neither statement combined the
printed residual with the source's full scope.  The repair supplied real and
complex unbounded statements with the candidate-subspace residual.  This case
shows why finding the right inequality or several related theorems was
insufficient: the hypotheses and conclusion had to occur together in a statement
covering the intended source claim.

\subsection{A review-driven over-correction}
\label{sec:overcorrection}

Theorem~8.1(ii) exposed a different problem.  It prints a
finite-dimensional block-eigenvalue inequality followed by ``and natural
infinite-dimensional extensions,'' but gives no extension formula
\citep{davis1970rotation}.  One review found that our treatment covered only the finite-dimensional
formula.  The resulting repair treated four approximation-number extensions as
the formal interpretation of the source phrase.  A subsequent review rejected
that choice because Section~1 admits more than one plausible infinite-dimensional
reading and Theorem~8.1 chooses none.  We retained the extensions as
generalizations and classified the phrase as underspecified.

These reviews distinguish finding missing source scope from selecting an
interpretation that the source does not fix.  The same boundary applies to proof
architecture: our alignment target concerns theorem statements in their
mathematical context.  For this case study, source-fidelity review therefore asks
whether the public theorem type has the source hypotheses, objects, scope,
constants, and conclusion, while allowing any correct Lean proof route.

\section{Conclusion}

\paragraph{Did we formalize Davis--Kahan?}
All 29 tracked results have checked formal treatments: Lean proofs for 28 and a
finite-dimensional counterexample plus proved repair for Proposition~4.4
(Appendix~\ref{app:data}).  Current source-to-signature review has found no
remaining material mismatch, but we have no calibrated stopping rule or formal
certificate that every source correspondence is correct.

This case study separates two checks that can otherwise be conflated.  Compiler
and kernel feedback establish that the encoded proposition is proved; source
review asks whether that proposition preserves the intended theorem, including
its hypotheses, mathematical objects, and scope.  The review history also shows
that this comparison must remain bounded by what the source actually says.
Further work is needed to determine how such review should be validated and when
it is sufficient.
