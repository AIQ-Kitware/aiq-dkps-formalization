\begin{abstract}
Coding agents can now produce substantial Lean developments for researchers who
are still learning to read Lean.  We ran into the resulting semantic-alignment
problem while trying to formalize all 29 mathematical results in Davis and
Kahan's 1970 paper.  We put our experience beside \PractitionerAccountCount{} public
first-person accounts of AI-assisted Lean work, where people report very
different answers to a basic question: how much Lean does the human need to
read?  We show one historical mismatch from our repository: a checked Section~2
$\tan\Theta$ theorem required condition~(3.5), which Davis and Kahan introduce
only in Section~3.  The repair replaces that caller-supplied condition with the
source's earlier definedness convention and derives (3.5) internally.  The
mathematical ledger resolves all 29 tracked results---28 by proof and
Proposition~4.4 by refutation plus repair.  A final signature-level review then
found seven public-signature/source-scope mismatches; those seven were repaired
on 6 September, with the final signature/context re-review still pending.  The
structured data, source notes, paper-revision prompts, and
reproduction scripts accompany the paper.
\ifshowartifacturl\space Public materials: \url{\artifacturl}.\fi
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Formalization adds another layer of trust to a mathematical argument: Lean
checks that a proof term establishes the proposition encoded in its theorem
statement \citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020}.  That guarantee reaches the
source theorem only when the formal proposition has the intended hypotheses,
objects, and scope.  In our project, formalizing Davis and Kahan's
Proposition~4.4 exposed that it is false as printed; we obtained a
finite-dimensional counterexample and a repaired statement.  Source comparison
later found a different problem in our own development: a
checked Section~2 $\tan\Theta$ theorem required a condition that Davis and Kahan
introduce only in Section~3.

Following FormalAlign \citep{lu2025formalalign}, we use \emph{semantic alignment}
for agreement between a formal proposition and the intended mathematical claim;
the same problem is also studied by ShadowBench \citep{han2026shadowbench}.  In
our case, the intended claim is expressed by Davis and Kahan's 1970 paper
together with the definitions, inherited assumptions, notation, and mathematical
context needed to read it.  Timothy Chow illustrates the distinction with
``doohickeys'': if the source says that all doohickeys are excellent while the
formal statement says only that special doohickeys are quasi-excellent, a checked
proof establishes only the latter statement \citep{chow2026mindreading}.

Effective formalization depends on reusable foundations: later projects can
build on checked definitions and theorems instead of reconstructing their
prerequisites.  Mathlib already provides a broad common base \citep{mathlib2020},
and Tau Ceti is one effort to
expand that base with AI-authored Lean \citep{tauceti2026}.  We wanted to test
whether coding agents could accelerate this kind of foundational development
while the first author was still learning Lean.  Davis--Kahan was a useful
target: we wanted reusable spectral-perturbation results for downstream
applications, and the 1970 paper ranges from matrices to Hilbert-space
operators \citep{davis1970rotation}.  We therefore set out to formalize all 29
mathematical results in the paper while developing reusable operator-theoretic
foundations along the way.

Once agents could produce Lean beyond the first author's unaided ability, a
successful compile established that Lean had proved the submitted statement.
We still had to determine whether the
submitted statement expressed the mathematics in the source.  \Cref{fig:workflow}
summarizes the workflow that grew around this problem: the source theorem and
its scope stay visible while agents decompose, formalize, compile, and review
candidate formalizations.

This question has become practical very quickly.  Papers With Lean indexed 174
Lean-related arXiv papers during 2025 and 410 during January--July 2026; July
alone contained 101 \citep{paperswithlean2026stats}.  The mean monthly rate over
those first seven months of 2026 was about \LeanPublicationRateRatio{} times the
2025 rate.  \Cref{fig:lean-activity} marks 10 July 2026, when a commit explicitly
scaffolded a ``full paper faithful DK theory.''  Davis--Kahan-specific work is
visible earlier; the 13 July commit was a later repository reorganization.
\Cref{fig:project-chronology,tab:review-timeline} separates these milestones and
extends the project history through 6 September 2026.

This paper studies how researchers supervise that source-to-formal-statement
correspondence when agents can write Lean beyond the human's own fluency.  We
compare our experience with public first-person reports and examine the
historical Section~2 mismatch above in detail.  The structured account notes
retain the public source, observation, qualification, and fields used for the
descriptive counts; the Git events used here are kept in a separate dated table
keyed by commit.  \Cref{app:data} describes the sources, validation checks, and
material hashes.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

We asked: when agents produce Lean beyond the user's own Lean fluency, what does
the human inspect or delegate before accepting that the formalization matches
the intended mathematics?  We read \PractitionerAccountCount{} public
first-person accounts from blogs, project transcripts, social posts, forums,
Zulip, and formal case studies.  We found them by web search and citation
chasing; representativeness is unknown.  \Cref{app:data} gives the per-account
source records, categorical-field schema, and build-time validation checks.
Collins et al. separately report a human-subject study of initial AI-assisted
formalization workflows \citep{collins2026workflows}.

\paragraph{How much Lean does the human need to read?}
The accounts do not agree.  Kahle and Saiki describe checking definitions,
hypotheses, and decomposition while agents write much of the proof code
\citep{kahle2026keeptrying,saiki2026double}.  Sergey describes stepping in at
representation choices and missing lemmas instead of repairing tactics line by
line \citep{sergey2026move}.  Suomela describes a workflow in which he does not
read the generated Lean before studying the mathematical proof, and Gowers
reports using Aristotle on a complicated paper without needing Lean knowledge
\citep{suomela2026workflow,gowers2026leiden}.  These accounts put the human at
very different distances from the formal code.

Some researchers use another model to mediate the semantic check.  Nowak and
F\"ugger used Claude to review Codex formalizations for added hypotheses,
weakening, and proof holes \citep{nowak2026workflow}.  Kova\v{c} asked another
model to translate generated Lean back into English because he could not read
it directly \citep{kovac2025erdos189}.  Ilin reports adversarial review among
multiple systems \citep{ilin2026vml}.  Counterexamples often provide a more
human-readable failure signal: Saiki used them to diagnose an underspecified
definition, and Oum corrected a source hypothesis after formalization
\citep{saiki2026double,oum2026preprint}.  Enomoto describes a later ``proof
digestion'' phase in which an already checked argument was reorganized until he
understood it mathematically \citep{enomoto2026essay}.

Within the \PractitionerAccountCount{} accounts, \AccountSemanticMismatchCount{}
explicitly report a statement, definition, scope, or correspondence mismatch,
with \AccountSemanticMismatchQualifiedCount{} additional qualified cases.
\AccountSeparateAIReviewCount{} describe a separate AI review role, and
\AccountPersistentStateCount{} keep substantive project state outside the chat.
Formalization exposed a defect in the informal source in
\AccountSourceDefectCount{} accounts; \AccountCounterexampleCount{} explicitly
used a counterexample to diagnose or communicate a problem.  These counts
describe the public accounts we found; representativeness is unknown.  The
structured rows and their source notes are included with the paper
(\cref{app:data}).

There is also a growing layer of tooling for the agent--Lean connection.
LeanDojo extracts proof states and premises and supports programmatic Lean
interaction \citep{yang2023leandojo}; Pantograph exposes the Lean frontend,
proof execution, expression construction, and environment inspection
\citep{aniva2024pantograph}; LeanInteract provides a Python interface to Lean's
REPL, including declaration and info-tree extraction \citep{poiroux2025leaninteract}.
For this project we built a smaller query tool, \texttt{leanq}, for asking the
pinned project environment for elaborated statements, declaration kinds,
source locations, axioms, and dependency information.
\ifshowartifacturl\space It is part of
\href{\leanqurl}{\texttt{aiq-lean-formalization-tools}}.\else\space It is included
in the supplementary code.\fi

Other tools focus on semantic alignment itself.  FormalAlign evaluates whether
an informal mathematical statement and its formalization agree in meaning
\citep{lu2025formalalign}.  EconCSLib places source text, Lean statements,
generated mathematical renderings, and human match/mismatch judgments together
\citep{garg2026econcs}.  Lean Atlas and Lean Compass prune proof-only dependencies
to expose a smaller set of declarations whose meanings can affect a target
theorem \citep{yanahama2026leanatlas}.  ShadowBench probes a formal statement
with auxiliary shadow statements and implication checks
\citep{han2026shadowbench}.  These systems are especially interesting for users
who cannot audit a large generated development line by line.

\section{The Davis--Kahan case}
\label{sec:dk}

\paragraph{Statement of the problem.}
At the matrix level, let $T=T^*$ be a Hermitian matrix and let $E_0$ have
orthonormal columns spanning a candidate (or trial) subspace $P$ for an invariant subspace
of $T$.  The Rayleigh--Ritz matrix
\[
  A_0 = E_0^* T E_0
\]
is the action of $T$ compressed to those coordinates.  The residual
\[
  R = T E_0 - E_0 A_0
\]
measures failure of invariance: $R=0$ exactly when applying $T$ to the trial
vectors stays inside their span.  If an exact spectral subspace $Q$ corresponds
to eigenvalues separated from the unwanted spectrum by a gap $\delta$, the
principal-angle operators $\Theta_0$ and $\Theta$ measure the rotation from $P$
to $Q$ in directed and ambient forms.

Davis and Kahan write the perturbed operator as $T=A+H$.  Under the ordered gap
and the Rayleigh--Ritz condition $H_0=0$, their Section~2 $\tan\Theta$ theorem
gives, for every unitary-invariant norm $N$,
\begin{equation}
  \delta N(\tan\Theta_0) \le N(R),
  \qquad
  \delta N(\tan\Theta) \le N(H).
  \label{eq:tantheta}
\end{equation}
The paper then carries this matrix picture to separable Hilbert spaces: matrices
become bounded or densely defined self-adjoint operators, $E_0$ becomes an
isometric embedding of the trial coordinates, and the same compression,
residual, spectral-gap, and angle relations are expressed without choosing an
ambient basis \citep{davis1970rotation}.

Our denominator of 29 results is explicit: the four unnumbered Section~2
headline theorems plus every named theorem, proposition, lemma, and corollary
that Davis and Kahan actually prove in the paper.  Definitions, proof steps,
examples, immediate consequences, and open or deferred claims are not separate
items in that count.  Some paper-independent operator theory developed along the
way is being prepared for possible contribution to Tau Ceti \citep{tauceti2026}.

The formalization also exposed a problem in the source itself.  Davis and
Kahan's Proposition~4.4 is false as printed.  The repository contains a
finite-dimensional counterexample and a repaired statement; the longer
formalization manuscript develops that result in detail \citep{aiqdkps2026repo}.

\subsection{Example of a semantically misaligned formalization}
\label{sec:false-finish}

The ambient conclusion in \cref{eq:tantheta} appears in Section~2.  Its printed
hypotheses are the ordered spectral gap, $\delta>0$, and the Rayleigh--Ritz
condition $H_0=0$.  At one point our canonical Lean endpoint additionally asked
the caller for
\texttt{h35 : CrossedDefectsEquivalent U V}.  That predicate formalizes
condition~(3.5), which Davis and Kahan introduce in Section~3 and then assume for
the remainder of the paper.  Requiring it in the Section~2 endpoint changed the
statement being formalized.

\lstinputlisting[style=leanpaper,
  caption={The relevant lines from the historical and repaired Section~2
  $\tan\Theta$ endpoints.  Full verbatim signatures, commit hashes, paths, and
  line ranges are included in the supplement.},
  label={lst:false-finish}]{generated/historical_false_finish.lean}

The repair uses an earlier convention from Section~1.  Davis and Kahan state
that a result is vacuous when a norm occurring in it does not exist.  For the
ambient tangent, existence means that no principal angle reaches $\pi/2$; the
formalization names this condition \texttt{HasDefinedAmbientTangent}.  From that
condition the repository proves
\texttt{crossedDefectsEquivalent\_of\_hasDefinedAmbientTangent}, so (3.5) can be
constructed inside the proof instead of appearing as a source hypothesis.  The
point is specific: the ordered gap and $H_0=0$ do not themselves supply
\texttt{h35}; the source's definedness convention supplies the premise from
which it is derived.

\section{Discussion}

Our review page places the source passage, inherited source context,
correspondence notes, and compiler-derived Lean statement together, backed by
\texttt{leanq} queries against the pinned environment and hashes of the reviewed
source and statement.  \Cref{fig:dashboard} shows the sine-theta page in the
appendix.  A reader who barely knows Lean still has to understand the structures,
predicates, coercions, and typeclasses in the signature.  The next step is
therefore theorem-centered definition inspection against the exact project and
Mathlib revisions, together with a Compass-like reduction to the vocabulary that
can affect the statement.

\paragraph{How much Lean did we actually read?}
At the start, the first author could not reliably audit a Lean theorem signature.
By the time of this paper, we could recognize several scope choices that had
caused trouble---real versus complex scalars, finite-dimensional assumptions,
bounded maps, and residual definitions---but still used LLMs and small query
tools to explain unfamiliar structures and follow definitions.  In the
Section~2 example in \cref{lst:false-finish}, the extra input is visible in the
signature; the harder part is checking that condition~(3.5) belongs only to the
later Section~3 scope and that the source's earlier definedness convention is
enough to derive it.  This gap between reading Lean syntax and reconstructing
source context motivated the review page.

\paragraph{What does 29/29 mean here?}
Our ledger tracks four unnumbered Section~2 theorems plus every named theorem,
proposition, lemma, and corollary.  Mathematically, 28 printed results have checked
proofs; Proposition~4.4 has a checked counterexample and repaired theorem.  A final
signature/context review found seven mismatches between public theorem signatures
and source scope, including extra caller hypotheses in a Theorem~8.2 residual
statement.  Those seven findings were subsequently repaired, but the final
signature/context re-review remained pending at the time of this revision.  Thus
29/29 mathematical resolution does not by itself establish 29/29 semantic
alignment.  Did we really formalize Davis--Kahan?  I don't know, probably. If not,
it's pretty close.  The
supplement preserves the human paper-revision prompts verbatim.
