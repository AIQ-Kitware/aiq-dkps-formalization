\section{A follow-on theorem enabled by the formal development}
\label{sec:example-transport}

We prove an exact formula for the smallest trace-norm displacement of a real
orthogonal map carrying one given subspace onto another. The formula is a
weighted-matching problem on their principal angles. Its proof uses the
principal-angle geometry, real and complex operator constructions, and
singular-value norms developed for the formalization, together with
additional optimization arguments.

Let $\like{Usource}{K},\like{Vtarget}{L}\subseteq\mathbb R^n$ have the same dimension and let
$\like{theta}{\theta_1},\ldots,\like{theta}{\theta_m}\in(0,\pi/2]$ be their positive principal angles,
counted with multiplicity.  A matching $\sym{Matching}$ on the angle labels consists
of disjoint unordered pairs; labels outside the pairs are unmatched.
Define the singleton and paired costs
\begin{equation}\label{eq:nuclear-matching-costs}
 \sym{SingletonCost}(\sym{theta})=\min\{4\sin(\sym{theta}/2),2\},\qquad
 \sym{PairCost}(\sym{theta},\like{theta}{\psi})=4\sin((\sym{theta}+\like{theta}{\psi})/2).
\end{equation}

A singleton represents transporting one principal plane independently.
Rotating it through $\sym{theta}$ costs $4\sin(\sym{theta}/2)$; an orthogonal
reflection carrying the same source line to the target costs $2$. Their
minimum is $\sym{SingletonCost}(\sym{theta})$. A matched pair allows the two principal planes to
interact: an orthogonal transporter on their four-dimensional sum attains
cost $\sym{PairCost}(\sym{theta},\like{theta}{\psi})$. Common zero-angle directions can remain fixed.

\begin{theorem}[Real trace-norm matching reduction]
\label{thm:real-matching-example}
For equal-dimensional real subspaces $\like{Usource}{K},\like{Vtarget}{L}$ as above,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:real-nuclear-match}
\min_{\substack{\sym{Wcompetitor}\in O(n)\\\sym{Wcompetitor}(\like{Usource}{K})=\like{Vtarget}{L}}}\|I-\sym{Wcompetitor}\|_1
=
\min_{\sym{Matching}\text{ a matching}}
\left(
\sum_{\{i,j\}\in \sym{Matching}}\sym{PairCost}(\like{theta}{\theta_i},\like{theta}{\theta_j})
+
\sum_{i\text{ unmatched}}\sym{SingletonCost}(\like{theta}{\theta_i})
\right).
\end{equation}
Both minima are attained.  The equality includes repeated angles,
right angles, and common ambient directions; the left side has no
principal-plane or determinant constraint.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof outline]
For $1<p<2$, minimize the displacement cost
$\like{PowerCost}{\Phi_p}(\sym{Wcompetitor})=\sum_j s_j(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})^p$ over the orthogonal transporters. The feasible
set is nonempty and compact, so a minimizer exists. The formal proof first
reduces such a minimizer to common invariant blocks for the source
projection, target projection, and transporter, of real dimension two or
four, together with fixed common directions.

The reduction uses first variations along $\sym{Wcompetitor}\exp(t\sym{SkewDirection})$. The skew-symmetric
spectral gradient $\sym{SpectralGradient}$ commutes with $\sym{Wcompetitor}$ and represents this derivative by
\[
 \left.\frac{d}{dt}\like{PowerCost}{\Phi_p}(\sym{Wcompetitor}\exp(t\sym{SkewDirection}))\right|_{t=0}
 =-\operatorname{tr}(\sym{SpectralGradient}\sym{SkewDirection})
\]
for skew-symmetric $\sym{SkewDirection}$. When $\sym{SkewDirection}$ commutes with the source projection $\sym{Psource}$,
the curve remains feasible. Stationarity for these directions gives
$\sym{Jreflect}\sym{SpectralGradient}\sym{Jreflect}=-\sym{SpectralGradient}$ for $\sym{Jreflect}=2\sym{Psource}-I$. On a positive spectral level $-\like{SpectralGradient}{G^2}=\like{GradientLevel}{g^2}I$,
the operator $\sym{SpectralGradient}/\sym{GradientLevel}$ squares to $-I$ and defines complex multiplication;
$\sym{Jreflect}$ acts as conjugation. On $0<t<\pi$, the scalar phase cost
$(2|\sin(t/2)|)^p$ has a derivative with at most two roots at each
positive level. Viewed in these complex coordinates, $\sym{Wcompetitor}$ therefore has
at most two phases. A real orthogonal block
decomposition of its Hermitian spectral projection gives the claimed
two- and four-dimensional real blocks. On the zero-gradient space, the
transporter is a reflection and the two-projection decomposition gives
blocks of dimension at most two. A one-dimensional $-1$ block can be
replaced by $+1$ without changing the transported subspace, so it cannot
occur at a minimum.

Replacing the transporter on one common invariant block preserves feasibility,
so a global minimizer restricts to a minimizer on each block.
Each two-dimensional block contributes a one-angle cost. Each
four-dimensional block contributes either two independent one-angle costs
or a paired cost. Principal-angle multisets and displacement costs add
over these common blocks, so their choices define a matching. Conversely,
any matching can be realized by taking the orthogonal sum of the
corresponding one- and two-plane transporters. This proves the matching
minimum for $1<p<2$.

Finally let $p$ decrease to $1$. In fixed finite dimension, $\like{PowerCost}{\Phi_p}(\sym{Wcompetitor})$
converges uniformly to $\|I-\sym{Wcompetitor}\|_1$ on the compact feasible set. The scalar
block minima also vary continuously with $p$, and there are only finitely
many matchings. Passing to the limit gives the displayed singleton and
pair costs and proves \cref{eq:real-nuclear-match}. Compactness gives an
attaining transporter at the endpoint.
\end{proof}

For $\like{theta}{\theta_1}=\like{theta}{\theta_2}=\pi/6$, the direct rotation costs
$8\sin(\pi/12)>2$, whereas the paired competitor costs
$4\sin(\pi/6)=2$. The theorem includes comparison with every ambient
orthogonal transporter, so this paired cost is globally minimal in the
example.

Davis and Kahan's complex-phase example already shows how a phase change
can decrease full trace displacement \citep[Example~4.2]{davis1970rotation}.
Bolotnikov, Li, and Rodman formulate the ambient unitary minimal-distortion
problem and prove Q-norm and Frobenius optimality results
\citep[Problem~1.1 and Theorems~3.1, 4.2]{bolotnikov2001minimal}. The formula
above specifies the unrestricted real trace-norm minimum through singleton
and paired angle costs. The real field permits pairing between distinct
principal planes, which is why the answer is an optimization over matchings.
