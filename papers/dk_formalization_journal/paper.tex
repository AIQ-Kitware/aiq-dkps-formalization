\begin{abstract}
We formalize the Davis--Kahan theory of spectral-subspace perturbations
from its 1970 primary source in Lean~4.  Of 29 reviewed result-level
statements, 28 are proved under their source hypotheses; the remaining
statement, Proposition~4.4, is refuted.  The development includes the
sine and tangent theorems, direct rotations, principal-angle operators,
Sylvester separation, unitarily invariant operator-ideal norms, and
unbounded self-adjoint operators.  We place these constructions in their
mathematical lineage, from the geometry of principal angles through
the theory of projections and the spectral methods underlying Davis
and Kahan's estimates.

The formalization required reusable operator theory, portions of which
were subsequently adapted into Tau Ceti, with additional foundations
adapted from Mathlib and the Spectra project.  Examining the full
displacement assertion in Proposition~4.4 produced a real
four-dimensional counterexample with arbitrarily small positive principal
angles.  We locate the failed singular-value comparison and prove a
corrected optimality statement for Q-norms.  The resulting infrastructure
also supports a later, separately proved theorem reducing nuclear-norm
optimal real subspace transport to weighted matching on principal
angles.  These outcomes show how developing the foundations of a
classical mathematical theory can support both a source correction and
new formal research.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When a self-adjoint operator is perturbed, an individual eigenvector may
be unstable even when the invariant subspace to which it belongs remains
well determined.  For instance, a multiple eigenvalue admits many
orthonormal eigenbases, while the span of the corresponding eigenvectors
is independent of that choice.  The Davis--Kahan theory quantifies the
stability of such subspaces through their principal angles, the
separation of relevant parts of the spectrum, and a perturbation or
computable residual \citep{davis1970rotation}.  This point of view
connects infinite-dimensional operator theory with the numerical
analysis of approximate eigenvectors.

The 1970 Davis--Kahan paper is an unusually rich formalization target.
It unifies the geometry of two closed subspaces with spectral
perturbation estimates, and frequently states conclusions for real and
complex Hilbert spaces, possibly unbounded self-adjoint operators, and
unitarily invariant norms on operator ideals.  A finite-matrix
specialization captures familiar sine-theta inequalities but omits
substantial parts of the source: domain conditions, half-infinite
spectral-gap configurations, and the behavior of operator ideals in
infinite dimensions.  The historical development of these mathematical
tools is traced in \cref{sec:related}.

Our Lean~4 formalization covers a reviewed set of 29 result-level
statements from the 1970 paper.  We prove 28 at their reviewed source
scope, including a sine-theta estimate for unbounded self-adjoint
operators and supported symmetric operator-ideal norms.  The remaining
statement, Proposition~4.4, asserts a false full-displacement
optimality property for direct rotation; a parameterized real
four-dimensional counterexample refutes it.  The complete source
inventory appears in Appendix~\ref{app:dk-inventory}.

To establish these statements, we developed and reused mathematical
interfaces for principal-angle operators, direct rotation, approximation
numbers, symmetric ideals, Borel spectral calculus, and domain-aware
Sylvester estimates.  Some foundations developed here were later
adapted into \href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau Ceti};
other parts were adapted from existing Mathlib and Spectra developments.
The formalization and its upstream contributions constitute a reusable
base for subsequent theorems.  In \cref{sec:example-transport} we give
one concrete example: the exact global minimum of nuclear displacement
under real orthogonal subspace transport is characterized by weighted
matching of principal angles.  This follow-on question arose from the
Proposition~4.4 investigation and uses the foundations developed during
the formalization effort.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A source-level formalization.} The 29-result review of
Davis--Kahan (1970) comprises 28 proved results and a formally refuted
Proposition~4.4.  The sine-theta theorem retains the real/complex
setting, unbounded self-adjoint domains, ordered as well as
interval/exterior gaps, and where-defined operator-ideal norms
(\cref{sec:coverage,sec:sine-theta}).
\item \textbf{An operator-theoretic library.} The proof develops
mathematical interfaces spanning the historical foundations of the
paper.  Parts of these interfaces were subsequently adapted into Tau
Ceti, with documented distinctions between adapted and independently
developed upstream implementations (\cref{sec:related,sec:foundations}).
\item \textbf{A correction to the 1970 source.} We give a real
four-dimensional counterexample at arbitrarily small positive angles,
identify the invalid Ky Fan comparison in Proposition~4.4, and prove a
valid Q-norm consequence of Proposition~4.3 (\cref{sec:prop44}).
\item \textbf{A formalization-enabled extension.} We exhibit a proved
reduction from unrestricted nuclear-optimal real transport to weighted
principal-plane matching (\cref{sec:example-transport}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{From subspace geometry to the Davis--Kahan estimates}
\label{sec:related}

The 1970 paper of Davis and Kahan is the point where several problems,
originally pursued for different reasons, become one theorem.  How does
one measure the relative position of two subspaces without selecting
bases?  How does one identify a subspace after the operator defining it
has changed?  What quantitative information does an approximate
eigenpair provide about the exact spectral subspace?  And how does one
measure error when the relevant operators act in infinite dimensions?
The answers came from geometry, spectral theory, numerical analysis,
and the theory of operator ideals, respectively.

The connecting question is simple to state.  Suppose a self-adjoint
operator $A$ has a distinguished spectral subspace, and $E_0$ describes
an approximate one.  The output we want is an estimate on their
\emph{relative geometry}, not a comparison of arbitrary eigenbases.
To obtain it, one first describes geometry using principal angles and
projections; then expresses failure of invariance as a residual;
then solves an operator equation whose coefficients are separated in
spectrum; and finally measures its solution in an appropriate
unitarily invariant norm.  The 1970 sine-theta bound is the point at
which those steps meet.

The historical literature is distributed across these mathematical
traditions.  Paige and Wei give a history of the cosine--sine
decomposition and its connections to Jordan, Davis, Kahan, and Stewart
\citep{paige1994history}.  Simon reconstructs the Sz.-Nagy--Kato
construction for nearby spectral projections and its role in
perturbation theory \citep[\S5]{simon2018kato}.  B\"ottcher and
Spitkovsky review the theory of two projections and its operator
algebraic extensions \citep{bottcher2010gentle}.  We use these accounts
together with the original works to follow the mathematical questions
through to the formalization.  The same historical objects recur in
the Lean development, which is why the mathematical lineage also
serves as a guide to its prerequisites.  A compact source-to-proof
map appears in \cref{tab:historical-lineage}.

\subsection{Angles before operators: Jordan, canonical correlation, and the
cosine--sine decomposition}
\label{sec:related-classical}

Camille Jordan studied the geometry of linear subspaces in $n$
dimensions in 1875 \citep{jordan1875essai}.  For two lines, a single
acute angle describes their relative position.  For subspaces of
higher dimension, one needs a sequence of \emph{principal angles}.
The successive principal vectors are selected under orthogonality
constraints, so their angles are independent of the coordinates used
to describe either subspace.  This separates the geometry of a
subspace from the arbitrary choice of its orthonormal basis.

One modern description begins with isometries $X$ and $Y$ whose ranges
are finite-dimensional subspaces $E$ and $F$.  The singular values of
the overlap matrix are the principal cosines:
\begin{equation}
  \sigma_j(X^*Y)=\cos\theta_j,
  \qquad 0\leq\theta_j\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.
  \label{eq:history-principal-cosines}
\end{equation}
The corresponding principal vectors $x_j\in E$ and $y_j\in F$ satisfy
$\langle x_i,y_j\rangle=\delta_{ij}\cos\theta_j$ in suitably oriented
coordinates.  For nonzero acute angles, each pair determines a real
two-dimensional plane in which the relative position is an ordinary
rotation.  An entire pair of subspaces is described by these planes,
together with their shared and orthogonal components.

The same geometric data appeared in statistics: Hotelling's 1936
canonical correlation analysis sought pairs of maximally correlated
linear combinations of two sets of variables
\citep{hotelling1936relations}.  After appropriate whitening, the
canonical correlations are singular values of a cross-coordinate map.
In numerical linear algebra, Bj\"orck and Golub developed algorithms
for computing principal angles and vectors in 1973
\citep{bjorck1973numerical}.  Their computational formulation makes
the basis-invariant angles available from orthonormal matrix
factorizations.

A further formulation uses the cosine--sine decomposition, or CSD, of
a partitioned orthogonal or unitary matrix.  The blocks of the
decomposition contain compatible diagonal cosine and sine factors.
Davis--Kahan gave much of the relevant subspace geometry, and Stewart
subsequently developed the CSD as a matrix factorization; Paige and
Wei describe that history and its connection to the direct rotation
\citep{paige1994history,stewart1977perturbation}.
This is more than a change of notation.  The same principal angles
can be obtained from an overlap matrix, a pair of projections, or a
block unitary decomposition.  The formalization uses the projection
and functional-calculus descriptions, while the finite-dimensional
counterexample uses principal-plane coordinates.


\subsection{The other nineteenth-century problem: approximate eigenvalues and modes}
\label{sec:related-ritz-weyl}

Jordan's angles answered how to compare subspaces once they are given.
An independent question came from mechanics and numerical analysis:
how can one approximate an eigenvalue or eigenmode when the underlying
differential equation cannot be solved exactly?  In the nineteenth
century Rayleigh estimated vibration frequencies using trial shapes
and their energy ratios \citep{rayleigh1877sound}.  Ritz developed a systematic finite-dimensional
variational approximation in the work published in 1909
\citep{ritz1909variations,leissa2005historical}.  The method selects
a trial space and studies the action of the operator through that
space.  This is a precursor to the trial-coordinate viewpoint that
Davis and Kahan later use in their residual bounds.

For a Hermitian matrix $A$, the Rayleigh quotient
$\langle Ax,x\rangle/\langle x,x\rangle$ expresses the scalar action
of $A$ along a trial vector.  The Courant--Fischer min--max
principle organizes the eigenvalues through extremal quotients over
subspaces \citep{bhatia1997matrix}.  Weyl's eigenvalue comparison
inequalities then control the motion of ordered eigenvalues under a
Hermitian perturbation \citep{weyl1912eigenwerte,bhatia1997matrix}.  In modern notation,
if $A$ and $B$ are Hermitian $n$-by-$n$ matrices and their eigenvalues
are arranged in the same order, then
\begin{equation}
  \max_j |\lambda_j(A)-\lambda_j(B)|\leq\|A-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}.
  \label{eq:history-weyl}
\end{equation}
This is an estimate on \emph{eigenvalues}; it does not tell us which
individual eigenvector of $A$ corresponds to which eigenvector of $B$.

The limitation already appears in two dimensions.  For any $\varepsilon>0$,
put
\begin{equation}
 A_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&\varepsilon\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 B_\varepsilon=\begin{pmatrix}\varepsilon&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}.
 \label{eq:history-swap-example}
\end{equation}
The matrices have identical ordered eigenvalues and
$\|A_\varepsilon-B_\varepsilon\|_{\mathrm{op}}=\varepsilon$,
yet their lowest-eigenvalue lines are perpendicular.  The gap of
each matrix is itself $\varepsilon$.  Thus the ratio of perturbation
size to spectral separation, not perturbation size alone, must enter
an eigenspace estimate.  For a cluster with repeated eigenvalues, an
entire invariant subspace is likewise more natural than a selected
orthonormal eigenbasis.

The analytic theory of this question developed through Rellich's
work on perturbations of self-adjoint operators and Kato's later
systematic account \citep{simon2018kato,kato1966perturbation}.
Rayleigh--Ritz approximation supplies a candidate subspace, Weyl-type
bounds describe spectral values, and principal angles describe the
missing geometric quantity.  Davis--Kahan provides the quantitative
connection between them.

\subsection{The passage to Hilbert space: the structure of two projections}
\label{sec:related-two-projections}

For a closed subspace of a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$, the orthogonal
projection remains a bounded operator even when the subspace is
infinite dimensional.  Denote the projections onto two closed
subspaces by $P$ and $Q$.  The positive compression $PQP$ on
$\operatorname{ran}P$ is the operator version of the squared overlap
matrix: in finite dimensions its eigenvalues are
$\cos^2\theta_j$.  In Hilbert space it need not have a discrete
spectrum.  Functional calculus nevertheless defines its positive
square root and the associated cosine and sine of an angle operator.

The relative position of two closed subspaces was studied by Dixmier
in 1948 and by Davis in his 1958 paper \emph{Separation of Two Linear
Subspaces} \citep{dixmier1948position,davis1958separation}.  Davis's
closeness and separation operators place the cosine/sine data in an
operator-theoretic setting.  Halmos's 1969 \emph{Two Subspaces} gives a
particularly transparent structural description
\citep{halmos1969two}.  One first splits off the four intersection
spaces corresponding to the combinations of membership in the ranges
and kernels of $P$ and $Q$.  On their remaining, generic part, the pair
is unitarily equivalent to
\begin{equation}
 P_{\mathrm{gen}}=\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad
 Q_{\mathrm{gen}}=\begin{pmatrix}C^2&CS\\CS&S^2\end{pmatrix},
 \qquad C^2+S^2=I,
 \quad CS=SC,
 \label{eq:history-halmos-block}
\end{equation}
where $C$ and $S$ are commuting positive contractions on a common
Hilbert space.  Thus an operator-valued angle replaces a finite list
of principal angles.  B\"ottcher and Spitkovsky survey this
representation and its consequences for the algebra generated by two
projections \citep{bottcher2010gentle}.

The four intersection spaces have a second role.  Directions in
$\operatorname{ran}P\cap\ker Q$ are orthogonal to the target subspace;
directions in $\ker P\cap\operatorname{ran}Q$ are orthogonal to the
source.  These are the right-angle defect spaces.  At acute angles
both disappear.  For a general pair they affect whether a specified
intertwining operator, in particular a direct rotation, exists.
The nonacute Section~3 assertions of Davis--Kahan retain these
conditions rather than replacing the Hilbert-space problem by a
finite matrix with a chosen basis.

There is a further reason this description belongs in the historical
chain.  A finite-dimensional orthogonal matrix admits a decomposition
into finitely many invariant planes.  In a general Hilbert space an
angle operator can instead have continuous spectrum.  The spectral
calculus of the pair of projections takes the place of a finite list
of angles, and the intersection and defect spaces cannot be discarded.
The formal direct-rotation results therefore use operator identities
that make sense without selecting principal vectors.  The familiar
finite-dimensional pictures are instances of that more general
construction.

\subsection{Sz.-Nagy and Kato: transporting nearby spectral subspaces}
\label{sec:related-projections}

Spectral perturbation leads naturally to a different question from
measuring the angle: if the projection $P$ of an isolated spectral
cluster changes to $Q$, can the two ranges be identified by a unitary
that depends continuously, or analytically, on the projections?
Sz.-Nagy developed the nearby-projection construction in work
published in 1947, with a Hungarian precursor from 1942
\citep{sznagy1947perturbations,simon2018kato}.  Kato gave a streamlined
algebraic construction, described in his 1955 report and later in
\emph{Perturbation Theory for Linear Operators}
\citep{kato1966perturbation,simon2018kato}.

For orthogonal projections the useful intertwiner is
\begin{equation}
 T=QP+(I-Q)(I-P), \qquad TP=QT.
 \label{eq:history-kato-T}
\end{equation}
The identity
\begin{equation}
 T^*T=I-(P-Q)^2
 \label{eq:history-kato-square}
\end{equation}
shows why $\|P-Q\|<1$ is the natural threshold: it makes $T^*T$
strictly positive and invertible.  Hence
\begin{equation}
 U=T\bigl(I-(P-Q)^2\bigr)^{-1/2},
 \qquad UPU^*=Q
 \label{eq:history-kato-unitary}
\end{equation}
is unitary.  This operator is the unitary polar factor of $T$.
In principal-plane coordinates it is the direct rotation through the
principal angle, acting trivially on common directions.

A crucial distinction is that many unitaries can satisfy $UPU^*=Q$.
The polar-factor formula selects one determined by the two projections
and continuously connected to the identity in the acute regime.  That
choice makes the construction valuable in perturbation theory: the
transport identifies moving spectral subspaces while avoiding an
arbitrary choice of frames.  It also makes the separate extremal
question in Davis and Kahan's Section~4 possible: under which
measures of displacement is this distinguished transporter actually
the least expensive one?

Kato used such transformations to identify nearby spectral
subspaces of analytic operator families, thereby reducing the study
of an isolated moving eigenvalue cluster to a fixed coordinate
space.  Simon's historical account explains the earlier Sz.-Nagy
construction, Kato's formula, and subsequent work on pairs of
projections \citep[\S5]{simon2018kato}.  In the formalization,
\cref{eq:history-kato-square,eq:history-kato-unitary} motivate the
polar-decomposition and square-root APIs used for Davis--Kahan's
existence, uniqueness, and symmetry theorems.  The small-angle
hypothesis is a geometric operator-norm condition, not an assumption
about the matrix dimensions.

\subsection{From the spectral theorem to perturbations of eigenspaces}
\label{sec:related-spectral}

The spectral theorem supplies a second way of obtaining a subspace:
select a region of the spectrum of a self-adjoint operator and take
the corresponding spectral projection.  The functional-calculus
framework developed from the work of Riesz, von Neumann, and others;
Kato made spectral projections central to the perturbation theory of
closed operators \citep{kato1966perturbation,simon2018kato}.
Unlike the finite-dimensional eigenspace picture, a spectral
subspace need not be spanned by a finite list of eigenvectors.
A self-adjoint operator may be unbounded, while each of its spectral
projections is bounded and everywhere defined.  Its domain is still
indispensable when the operator acts on a trial vector or when an
intertwining identity is asserted.

For an isolated spectral cluster, the projection can be recovered
from a contour integral of the resolvent.  Using a positively oriented
contour $\Gamma$ in the resolvent set enclosing the cluster, the
complex case has the representation
\begin{equation}
 P_\Gamma=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_\Gamma(zI-A)^{-1}\,dz.
 \label{eq:history-riesz-projection}
\end{equation}
The resolvent is bounded at each point of $\Gamma$ even when $A$ is
unbounded.  This makes the spectral projection a stable object for
perturbation analysis, while preserving the separate domain
requirements whenever $A$ itself appears in an identity.  More
general Borel spectral projections cover intervals and half-lines,
including spectral configurations for which a single isolated
finite-eigenvalue cluster is not the right model.  These are among
the reasons the Lean development needs Borel functional calculus,
not just diagonalization of finite matrices.

The Courant--Fischer min--max principle and Weyl-type eigenvalue
perturbation bounds provide complementary information about spectral
\emph{values} \citep{bhatia1997matrix,kato1966perturbation}.  They do
not by themselves identify a stable basis inside a multiple-eigenvalue
cluster, or quantify the movement of its invariant subspace.  This
separation between eigenvalue error and subspace error helped shape
Davis's perturbation program.  It also connects to the Courant--Fischer
and Weyl interfaces developed in the present formalization effort and
subsequently adapted into Tau Ceti.

Davis studied the rotation of eigenvectors under perturbation in
1963 and 1965 \citep{davis1963rotation,davis1965rotation}.  The
1969 Davis--Kahan announcement preceded their extended 1970 treatment
\citep{daviskahan1969bounds,davis1970rotation}.  Their problem has
two closely related formulations.  In one, the original self-adjoint
operator is perturbed and the movement of an exact spectral subspace
is estimated.  In the other, one has an approximate trial subspace and
a computable residual: how far can it be from the desired exact
spectral subspace?  Unlike a comparison of individual eigenvectors,
these are meaningful even when an eigenvalue has multiplicity and its
orthonormal eigenbasis is nonunique.

Let $E_0$ embed the trial-coordinate Hilbert space into the ambient
space isometrically, and let $A_0$ be a self-adjoint trial operator.
The residual is the bounded operator agreeing, where defined, with
\begin{equation}
 R=AE_0-E_0A_0.
 \label{eq:history-residual}
\end{equation}
It measures the failure of the trial coordinates to intertwine with
the ambient operator $A$.  A second isometric coordinate map for the
desired exact spectral space defines its orthogonal projection $P$.
The positive operator
\begin{equation}
 \sin\Theta_0=\bigl(I-E_0^*PE_0\bigr)^{1/2}
 \label{eq:history-angle-sine}
\end{equation}
measures the component of a trial vector lying outside the exact
subspace.  The critical distinction for the formal proof is that this
positive sine acts on the trial-coordinate space, whereas
$(I-P)E_0$ maps into the ambient space.  Their moduli coincide, so
unitarily invariant ideal norms can compare them without conflating
their domains and codomains.

\subsection{Sylvester and Rosenblum: the analytic mechanism of the angle bounds}
\label{sec:related-sylvester}

The matrix equation $BX-XA=Y$ is associated with Sylvester's 1884
work \citep{sylvester1884equation}.  Rosenblum studied separated
operator spectra in 1956 and later the equation with self-adjoint
coefficients \citep{rosenblum1956equation,rosenblum1969selfadjoint}.
The key idea for spectral perturbation is that separation of the
actions on two blocks controls the operator joining those blocks.

The scalar prototype is $(b-a)x=y$: if $|b-a|\geq\delta>0$,
then $\delta|x|\leq|y|$.  In the operator problem the scalar
multipliers are replaced by the spectral actions of two operators.
The difficulty is to preserve the appropriate lower bound when the
operators act on different spaces, may be unbounded, and the error
is measured by a singular-value norm rather than one scalar absolute
value.  The Sylvester theorems provide exactly this analytic step.

Let $F_1$ give isometric coordinates for the unwanted complementary
spectral subspace, with self-adjoint block $\Lambda_1$.  Writing
$X=F_1^*E_0$ converts the residual identity into a Sylvester equation:
\begin{equation}
 \Lambda_1X-XA_0=F_1^*R
 \label{eq:history-sylvester}
\end{equation}
on the appropriate domain.  Thus $X$ describes the geometric leakage
of the trial space into the unwanted block, while the right side is
controlled by the computable residual.  Under the interval/exterior
or ordered spectral-gap hypotheses of the sine-theta theorem,
separated Sylvester estimates give
\begin{equation}
 \delta\,N(X)\leq N(F_1^*R)\leq N(R).
 \label{eq:history-separation-bound}
\end{equation}
This is the bridge from a spectral-gap assumption to the bound on the
sine of a subspace angle.

The precise separation configuration matters.  A gap between two
arbitrary interlaced spectra does not by itself establish the same
constant-one inequality for every unitarily invariant norm.  Davis
and Kahan treat particular ordered and interval/exterior positions;
the full-scope Lean theorem retains those alternatives, including
cases where both operators are unbounded.  A formal implementation
therefore needs the domains of unbounded self-adjoint operators,
their spectral projections, strong spectral cutoffs, and the
ideal-norm inequalities used to pass through bounded approximations.

\subsection{Von Neumann, Schatten, Ky Fan, and the choice of displacement cost}
\label{sec:related-norms}

The geometry of a pair of subspaces is fixed, but there are many ways
to assign a cost to a unitary transporting one onto the other.
Von Neumann's study of unitarily invariant matrix norms identified
the role of symmetric gauge functions of singular values
\citep{vonneumann1937matrix}.  Schatten developed normed operator
ideals, while Ky Fan's eigenvalue and singular-value inequalities
provided the partial-sum comparisons underlying majorization
arguments \citep{schatten1950crossspaces,fan1951maximum,bhatia1997matrix}.
For a matrix $Z$, the Schatten norms include
\begin{equation}
 \|Z\|_{S_p}=\left(\sum_j s_j(Z)^p\right)^{1/p},
 \qquad
 \|Z\|_{S_1}=\sum_j s_j(Z),
 \quad
 \|Z\|_{S_2}=\left(\sum_j s_j(Z)^2\right)^{1/2}.
 \label{eq:history-schatten}
\end{equation}
In infinite dimensions these expressions are finite on their
corresponding operator ideals; outside an ideal they need not define
a finite norm.  This is why the 1970 paper's ``where defined''
qualification belongs to the mathematical hypotheses of its
norm-general theorems.

The largest singular value gives the operator norm, the Euclidean
norm of the singular-value sequence gives the Hilbert--Schmidt norm,
and its sum gives the nuclear norm.  These functionals measure
different aspects of the same displacement.  Concentrating motion
into fewer singular directions can lower the nuclear sum while
increasing the largest displacement or its squared sum.  The
connection to Ky Fan majorization is therefore a question about the
\emph{whole ordered singular-value sequence}, not merely about
rotating each principal plane through its smallest angle.

Davis established Hilbert--Schmidt and finite-dimensional Schatten
extremal properties for exponents at least two in his 1958 subspace
paper \citep[Theorems~7.3--7.4]{davis1958separation}.  Davis and
Kahan's Section~4 distinguishes a restricted displacement, a
squared full displacement, and the full displacement $I-W$ of an
ambient transporter.  Those are different optimization objectives.
They exhibited a complex-phase modification that decreases the nuclear
norm of full displacement \citep[Example~4.2]{davis1970rotation}.
In contrast, Proposition~4.4 asserts that direct rotation minimizes
full displacement for real subspaces if their principal angles are no
larger than $\pi/3$.  The four-dimensional example in
\cref{sec:prop44} gives a smaller trace norm at every positive acute
angle.  It exposes the failed step: adding singular-value sums of
restrictions to separate principal planes is not a valid global lower
bound for an arbitrary ambient transporter.

The subsequent minimal-distortion theory of Bolotnikov, Li, and Rodman
studied the same class of subspace-transport constraints, including
Frobenius and Q-norm optimality and explicit subquadratic
counterexamples \citep{bolotnikov2001minimal}.  Later work on
Grassmannian metrics and canonical-angle inequalities also separates
intrinsic paths between subspaces from minimizing the extrinsic
operator displacement \citep{qiu2006grassmann,zhang2010canonicalangles}.
These results give the norm-theoretic context for the source
correction and for the nuclear-matching question described in
\cref{sec:example-transport}.


\subsection{The 1969--1970 synthesis: an angle estimate from an observable residual}
\label{sec:related-synthesis}

The historical strands met in Davis's earlier papers on the rotation
of eigenvectors in 1963 and 1965, the 1969 Davis--Kahan announcement,
and the extended 1970 paper
\citep{davis1963rotation,davis1965rotation,daviskahan1969bounds,davis1970rotation}.
The contribution of that synthesis is easiest to see by following the
argument from the given numerical data to the geometric conclusion.
One begins with an approximate coordinate map $E_0$ and a trial
operator $A_0$.  Their failure to describe an invariant subspace is
measured by the residual $R=AE_0-E_0A_0$.  The target spectral
projection $P$ then singles out the unwanted component $(I-P)E_0$.
When an isometric map $F_1$ represents that complementary spectral
subspace, the off-diagonal coordinate operator $X=F_1^*E_0$ obeys
\cref{eq:history-sylvester}.  The spectral gap controls $X$ through
the separated Sylvester equation.  Finally, the singular values of
$X$ are those of the positive sine of the principal-angle operator,
up to the zero-padding appropriate to rectangular operators.

The resulting chain of equalities and inequalities is
\begin{equation}
 \underbrace{\delta N(\sin\Theta_0)}_{\text{subspace error}}
 =\delta N(X)
 \leq N(F_1^*R)
 \leq\underbrace{N(R)}_{\text{computable residual}}.
 \label{eq:history-unified-chain}
\end{equation}
Each equality has its own mathematical ancestry: the first is
principal-angle and polar-decomposition geometry; the middle estimate
is a Sylvester separation theorem; and the final bound uses ideal
norm properties.  This also explains the architecture of the formal
proof.  The objects have different domains and codomains, the
unbounded operators require domain hypotheses, and the norm may be
finite only on a specified ideal.

Davis and Kahan also developed tangent and double-angle estimates,
direct-rotation extremal statements, generalizations beyond a
single spectral cluster, and branch-selection results.  These are
not independent curiosities: the tangent bounds use different
geometric coordinates, while branch selection establishes which
angle regime the spectral assumptions permit.  Section~4 of their
paper returns to the unitary transporter itself and asks whether it
minimizes displacement.  That question leads to the source
correction examined in \cref{sec:prop44}.  The 1970 article thus
joins a perturbation theory of subspaces with a geometric and
norm-dependent theory of the transformations relating them.

\subsection{The numerical and statistical legacy after 1970}
\label{sec:related-numerical}

The 1970 theory influenced more than the analysis of exact
self-adjoint eigenvectors.  Wedin's 1972 singular-subspace
perturbation bounds address the corresponding problem for singular
vectors of general rectangular matrices
\citep{wedin1972perturbation}.  Bj\"orck and Golub's angle algorithms
made the subspace quantities computable
\citep{bjorck1973numerical}.  Stewart's work on pseudoinverses,
projections, and least-squares sensitivity helped connect these
geometric notions with matrix computations
\citep{stewart1977perturbation,stewart1990matrix}.
Paige and Wei's 1994 history shows how the cosine--sine decomposition
links the principal-angle geometry of Davis and Kahan with numerical
matrix factorizations \citep{paige1994history}.

The same subspace-versus-eigenvector distinction remains central in
statistical spectral estimation.  For example, Yu, Wang, and
Samworth analyze a version of Davis--Kahan particularly suited to
estimating eigenspaces from data \citep{yu2015davis}, while Chen and
collaborators survey spectral methods across statistical learning
problems \citep{chen2021spectral}.  These are applications and
adaptations of perturbation theory, rather than predecessors of the
1970 proof.  Their dependence on stable subspaces explains the value
of a reusable formal theorem stated at the operator level rather than
for one matrix dimension or one matrix norm.

\subsection{Historical foundations as reusable formal mathematics}
\label{sec:related-formal}

Each historical component above corresponds to a specific requirement
in the formalization.  Jordan's angle geometry and Halmos's
projection blocks lead to positive principal-angle operators rather
than chosen eigenbases.  The Sz.-Nagy--Kato intertwiner requires
polar decomposition, square roots, and real/complex transfer.
Spectral projections require Borel functional calculus, including for
unbounded self-adjoint operators.  The Sylvester proof requires a
representation carrying operator domains and a controlled passage
through spectral cutoffs.  Schatten and Ky Fan theory requires
approximation numbers and symmetric operator ideals to express
inequalities with the source's norm generality.

The implementation uses Lean~4 and Mathlib
\citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020,dedecker2025cfc}.
Projection-valued-measure and Borel-calculus constructions also use
material adapted from Adam Bornemann's \emph{Spectra} formalization
\citep{bornemann2026spectra}.  Several foundations developed during
the Davis--Kahan effort were subsequently adapted into the
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau Ceti Lean library}.
The \href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/13599}{Courant--Fischer
and Weyl contribution} and
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/14241}{rectangular
unitarily invariant seminorm contribution} record DKPS donor
adaptations; the
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/17527}{projection-valued
measure contribution} has Spectra ancestry.  Other upstream
contributions, including canonical polar decomposition, were proved
independently there.  These distinctions concern the provenance of
formal implementations; the mathematical statements retain their
classical attributions.

This history also separates the two mathematical questions that meet
in the project.  The sine-theta theorem estimates \emph{how far} an
approximate or perturbed spectral subspace lies from an exact one.
The direct-rotation problem asks \emph{which} ambient transformation
carries one given subspace onto another at minimum displacement cost.
The latter optimization problem is not derived from the sine-theta
inequality.  It became accessible in the same formal development
because its principal-angle, projection, and operator-ideal
foundations had already been built.  The resulting Proposition~4.4
counterexample and the follow-on matching theorem are examined in
\cref{sec:prop44,sec:example-transport}.

These results depend on spectral and operator-ideal constructions that cannot

\subsection{The full-scope sine-theta theorem}
\label{sec:sine-theta}

\subsubsection{Trial and exact subspaces}

Davis and Kahan distinguish a reference operator $\sym{A}$ from a perturbed operator
$\sym{A}+\sym{H}$.  For the formal statement we write $\sym{Acurrent}$ for the perturbed full-space
operator, corresponding to the source's $\sym{A}+\sym{H}$.

Let $\sym{Eambient}$ be the ambient Hilbert space.  Let $\sym{A0}$ be a self-adjoint operator on a
trial-coordinate space $\sym{Ftrial}$, and let
\[
  \sym{E0}:\sym{Ftrial}\to \sym{Eambient}
\]
be an isometric coordinate map for the trial subspace.  The residual compares
applying the ambient operator after embedding with applying the trial operator
before embedding:
\begin{equation}
  \sym{R} = \sym{Acurrent}\sym{E0}-\sym{E0}\sym{A0}.
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
When the operators are unbounded, \cref{eq:residual} is asserted on
$\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$ and $\sym{R}$ is the bounded residual that agrees there.
The Lean predicate \texttt{IsTrialResidual} packages the isometry, the required
domain inclusion, and this residual identity.

Let $\sym{F0}:\sym{Hexact}\to \sym{Eambient}$ be isometric coordinates for the desired exact spectral
subspace and let $\sym{F1}:\sym{Gunwanted}\to \sym{Eambient}$ be isometric coordinates for its orthogonal
complement.  A self-adjoint operator $\sym{Lambda1}$ on $\sym{Gunwanted}$ describes the
complementary spectral block through
\[
  \sym{Acurrent}\sym{F1} = \sym{F1}\sym{Lambda1}
\]
on its domain.  The predicate \texttt{IsExactSpectralDecomposition} also records
orthogonality and completeness of the two coordinate ranges.

\subsubsection{The sine operator and its rectangular representative}

The source angle operator acts on the trial-coordinate space.  If
$\sym{Pexact}=\sym{F0}\like{F0}{F_0^*}$ is the orthogonal projection onto the desired exact subspace, then
\[
  \cos\sym{Theta0} = (\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0})^{1/2},
  \qquad
  \sym{sinTheta0} = (I-\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0})^{1/2}.
\]
The analytic proof uses the rectangular complementary block
\begin{equation}
  \sym{S}=(I-\sym{Pexact})\sym{E0}.
  \label{eq:rectangular-sine}
\end{equation}
The source-facing Lean theorem is stated on a positive operator on the
trial-coordinate space.  The rectangular block has codomain $\sym{Eambient}$, while the
positive sine has codomain $\sym{Ftrial}$.  Their relationship is
\[
  \sym{S}^*\sym{S}
  = \like{E0}{E_0^*}(I-\sym{Pexact})\sym{E0}
  = I-\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0},
\]
so
\begin{equation}
  |\sym{S}|=(\sym{S}^*\sym{S})^{1/2}=\sym{sinTheta0}.
  \label{eq:modulus-sine}
\end{equation}
The Lean API constructs the directed angle on trial coordinates by functional
calculus and defines its sine by applying $\sin$ through the same calculus.
Under the isometry hypotheses, Lean proves that this operator is exactly the
modulus $|\sym{S}|$ in \cref{eq:modulus-sine}.  A polar decomposition $\sym{S}=\sym{PolarU}|\sym{S}|$ and
the ideal laws then imply
\[
  \sym{NormN}(\sym{S})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})
\]
whenever the norm is defined.  The rectangular theorem is the analytic proof
engine; the selected source-facing theorem transports its conclusion to the
positive sine operator.

\subsubsection{Gap geometry and the inequality}

Fix $\sym{delta}>0$. The separation hypothesis has the following alternatives.
For some $\sym{betaEndpoint}\leq\sym{alphaEndpoint}$, one of the two spectra
$\sigma(\sym{A0})$ and $\sigma(\sym{Lambda1})$ lies in $[\sym{betaEndpoint},\sym{alphaEndpoint}]$ and the other
lies in
\[
 (-\infty,\sym{betaEndpoint}-\sym{delta}]\;\cup\;[\sym{alphaEndpoint}+\sym{delta},\infty).
\]
Alternatively, for some real $b$, the operator forms satisfy either
\[
 \sym{A0}\geq (b+\sym{delta})I,\quad \sym{Lambda1}\leq bI,
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 \sym{A0}\leq bI,\quad \sym{Lambda1}\geq (b+\sym{delta})I.
\]
Each form inequality is asserted on the domain of the corresponding operator;
for example, $\sym{A0}\geq aI$ means
$\langle \sym{A0}x,x\rangle\geq a\|x\|^2$ for every
$x\in\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$. For self-adjoint operators the source's ordered
spectral half-line hypotheses imply these form bounds. The Lean predicate
\texttt{FormBoundedSylvesterGap} records precisely the finite-interval
alternative, in either spectral order, and these two ordered form alternatives.

Both blocks may be unbounded in an ordered configuration. For instance,
on $\ell^2(\mathbb N_{\geq1})$, take diagonal operators
$\sym{A0}e_n=(n+\sym{delta})e_n$ and $\sym{Lambda1}e_n=-ne_n$, on the domains where their
images are square summable. They satisfy the first ordered configuration
with $b=0$, while neither spectrum lies in a finite interval. This example
explains why retaining the half-infinite alternatives changes the theorem's
applicability.

The source also writes a common-domain equality for the unbounded trial
configuration.  That equality discharges the forward domain condition consumed
by \texttt{IsTrialResidual}.  Source correspondence is checked from the combined
hypotheses, with this equality supplying the required domain condition.

Appendix~\ref{app:lean-guide} explains the general Lean syntax in the
following theorem signature; the geometric and spectral hypotheses have been
defined above.

\begin{theorem}[Sine-theta estimate]
\label{thm:sine-theta}
Let the scalar field be $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, let $\sym{Eambient}$ be separable,
and let $\sym{Acurrent}$, $\sym{A0}$, and $\sym{Lambda1}$ be self-adjoint operators on the
Hilbert spaces above. Suppose the isometries, domain inclusions, residual
identity, exact complementary decomposition, and one of the three gap
configurations hold. Let $\sym{NormN}$ be a supported normalized symmetric
operator-ideal norm. Whenever $\sym{sinTheta0}$ and $\sym{R}$ belong to its domain,
\begin{equation}
 \sym{delta}\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R}).
 \label{eq:dk-sine}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

The two ideal-membership hypotheses express the ``where-defined'' restriction.
The proof first estimates the rectangular map $\sym{S}=(I-\sym{F0}\like{F0}{F_0^*})\sym{E0}$ and then
uses \cref{eq:modulus-sine} and modulus invariance of the ideal gauge. The
ambient Hilbert-space instances are omitted from the displayed Lean signature.
The theorem contains all three separation configurations encoded by its gap
hypothesis.  The ordered half-infinite cases have distinct hypotheses from
the finite interval/exterior specialization, so source correspondence is checked
against the complete family of gap hypotheses.

\subsubsection{Why the Sylvester estimate gives the angle bound}

Set $\sym{X}=\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{E0}$. The exact complementary decomposition gives
$\sym{S}=\sym{F1}\sym{X}$, and multiplication by the isometry $\sym{F1}$ preserves the supported
singular-value gauges. Self-adjointness, the domain hypotheses, and the
residual identity yield the Sylvester relation
\[
 \sym{Lambda1} \sym{X}-\sym{X}\sym{A0}=\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{R}
\]
on $\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$, with the left side represented by the bounded
right side. The separated Sylvester estimate gives
\[
 \sym{delta} \sym{NormN}(\sym{X})\leq \sym{NormN}(\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{R})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R}).
\]
Combining this with $\sym{NormN}(\sym{X})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{S})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})$ proves the conclusion.
For unbounded operators, deriving the domain compatibility for $\sym{X}$ is part of
the argument; the displayed relation is not an everywhere-defined composition
of unbounded maps. The constant-one estimate uses the specified interval or
ordered gap geometry, rather than an arbitrary assertion that the two spectra
are disjoint.


For sharpness in one dimension, take
$\sym{Acurrent}=\operatorname{diag}(0,\sym{delta})$, the trial scalar
$\sym{A0}=0$, and the unit vector
$\like{v}{v_\theta}=(\cos\sym{theta},\sin\sym{theta})$.
The complementary component has norm $\sin\sym{theta}$ and the
residual has norm $\sym{delta}\sin\sym{theta}$.  Thus equality holds
in the sine-theta estimate for this finite-dimensional configuration.

\section{Reusable operator-theoretic foundations}
\label{sec:foundations}
\label{sec:dk-rest}

The proofs draw on three closely related areas: the geometry of two
subspaces, symmetric operator ideals and majorization, and Sylvester
estimates for spectrally separated operators.

\subsection{Direct rotations and the geometry of two subspaces}

Let $\sym{Psource}$ and $\sym{Qtarget}$ be the orthogonal projections onto closed subspaces $\sym{Usource}$ and
$\sym{Vtarget}$.  Davis and Kahan organize their geometry through the operators obtained
from $\sym{Psource}$, $\sym{Qtarget}$, and their complements.  In the acute case, the compression
$\sym{Psource}\sym{Qtarget}\sym{Psource}$ has no kernel on $\sym{Usource}$ and determines the cosine of the angle operator;
the complementary compression determines its sine.  Polar decomposition then
supplies the partial isometries that pair the corresponding principal
subspaces.  Combining these pieces gives the direct rotation, an orthogonal or
unitary operator $\sym{Rrotation}$ satisfying $\sym{Rrotation}(\sym{Usource})=\sym{Vtarget}$ and rotating along the principal
planes.

This construction explains several results that look separate when stated only
at theorem level.  Existence and uniqueness in the acute case, the
principal-square-root characterization, commutation with the angle operators,
and reversal symmetry all express compatibility among the same projection and
polar-decomposition data.  The nonacute theory records the additional kernel
conditions needed when principal angles reach $\pi/2$.  For compact angle
operators, the resulting decomposition reduces to the familiar sequence of
principal angles; the general Hilbert-space statements do not require such a
discrete spectrum.

Section~8 returns to this geometry from the spectral side.  Perturbation
smallness and spectral repulsion rule out the wrong branch and place the two
spectral subspaces in the acute regime where the direct rotation is available.
In the formal development this branch-selection argument uses the same
unbounded spectral calculus as the perturbation theorems.  Acuteness therefore
follows from the spectral hypotheses, with no separate matrix-level assumption.

\subsection{Approximation numbers, majorization, and unitarily invariant norms}

Davis and Kahan formulate many extremal statements simultaneously for broad
classes of unitarily invariant norms.  In finite dimensions such a norm can be
described by a symmetric gauge function of the singular values.  The
infinite-dimensional formalization uses approximation numbers as the singular
value sequence of a compact operator and symmetric norming functions to encode
the gauge.  Ky Fan partial sums then provide the bridge from pointwise or
majorization statements to norm inequalities.

This layer is essential in the extremal displacement argument. If $\sym{Rrotation}$ is the direct rotation and $\sym{Wcompetitor}$
is any unitary carrying $\sym{Usource}$ onto $\sym{Vtarget}$, the restricted displacement has
singular-value extremality strong enough to imply
unitarily invariant norm minimality.  Davis and Kahan also prove a
majorization theorem for the squared full displacement
$(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})^*(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})$.  Squaring changes the relevant singular values from
$s_j(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})$ to $s_j(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})^2$, so a majorization statement at the squared level
does not automatically imply the corresponding statement for the unsquared
trace norm.  Proposition~4.4 attempts precisely that stronger passage. The
counterexample in Section~\ref{sec:prop44} shows where it fails; the
$\sym{NormQ}$-norm assertion and quantitative bound in
Section~\ref{sec:prop44} give two valid conclusions.

The same operator-ideal machinery is used in the perturbation inequalities.
For the sine theorem, the statement $\sym{delta}\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{S})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R})$ is meaningful for
any supported symmetric operator ideal once both $S$ and the residual belong to
that ideal.  The formal theorem therefore keeps the source's ``where-defined''
convention and does not build an accidental finite-dimensional compactness
assumption into the statement.

\subsection{Sylvester separation and unbounded operators}

The analytic engine behind the sine and tangent estimates is the Sylvester map
\[
  \sym{X} \longmapsto AX-XB.
\]
Under the interval/exterior or ordered spectral-gap configurations used above,
Davis and Kahan obtain lower bounds of the form
\[
  \delta\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{X}) \leq \sym{NormN}(AX-XB).
\]
The perturbation theorems result by identifying the appropriate off-diagonal or
trial-subspace operator $\sym{X}$ and rewriting its Sylvester residual in terms of the
original perturbation.

The unbounded case forces domain information into this argument.  Expressions
such as $AX-XB$ are only meaningful on the appropriate domains, and the
spectral separation may be encoded by a finite interval/exterior configuration
or by an ordered pair of half-lines.  The reusable development represents
unbounded operators as domain-carrying linear partial maps, develops the
self-adjoint and semibounded interfaces needed by the proof, and connects
spectral hypotheses to a form-bounded Sylvester gap.  Strong spectral cutoffs
reduce parts of the argument to bounded operators; convergence of the relevant
approximation numbers then permits passage back to the unbounded setting.

Section~6 reuses this engine after weakening the trial-coordinate assumptions.
Reflection pinching and direct-sum norm comparisons control the resulting block
operators, while the Sylvester estimate supplies the spectral-gap factor.  This
produces the generalized sine and tangent theorems and the pairwise-gap
square-norm estimate at the scope stated in the source.

\subsection{Formal foundations and ancestry}

The analytic and geometric ingredients above required interfaces not
previously available in sufficient generality in the project's pinned
Lean dependencies.  The development adds the principal-angle and
projection constructions, approximation numbers and symmetric
operator ideals, polar decomposition and direct rotations, Borel
spectral calculus, unbounded self-adjoint partial maps, and real/complex
transfer.  Complexification allows spectral arguments to be developed
using complex functional calculus and then specialized to real Hilbert
spaces when the historical statement covers both fields.

Mathlib provides the basic algebraic, topological, Hilbert-space, and
continuous-functional-calculus framework
\citep{mathlib2020,dedecker2025cfc}.  Projection-valued-measure and
Borel-calculus constructions also use ideas adapted from Bornemann's
Spectra development \citep{bornemann2026spectra}.  Several DKPS donor
results were subsequently integrated into the
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau Ceti source
library}; other operator-theory roadmap results were proved

The mathematical ancestry of these interfaces is described in
\cref{sec:related-formal}.

\section{Discussion: what the formalization made accessible}
\label{sec:discussion}

The formalization brings together the geometric, spectral, and
norm-theoretic components of the 1970 paper in one reusable setting.
Its sine-theta statement includes ordered half-infinite gap cases and
unbounded self-adjoint domains; its direct-rotation results treat the
operator geometry of closed Hilbert subspaces; and its operator-ideal
layer supports norm comparisons beyond a single finite-dimensional
matrix norm.  These choices matter mathematically because changing
the scalar field, restricting to finite matrices, or suppressing the
domain of an unbounded operator changes which theorem has been proved.

Proposition~4.4 illustrates a different benefit of source-level
formalization.  Proposition~4.3 controls a squared-displacement
quantity, but a sum-of-singular-values inequality for the unsquared
displacement requires an additional argument.  The explicit
counterexample demonstrates that this further implication fails.  In
the positive direction, the Q-norm result identifies a class of costs
for which the squared-displacement theorem does establish direct-
rotation optimality.  The distinction is visible in the singular
values of the real four-dimensional example, rather than only in the
outcome of a proof search.

The new real nuclear matching theorem in \cref{sec:example-transport}
uses the same principal-angle, real/complex, and operator-norm
infrastructure.  It is a further result of the formalization research
program: the original source audit made the transport question
concrete, and the resulting library supported its formal investigation.
The separate spectral-transport manuscript develops the stronger
classification and infinite-dimensional results.  For the
formalization paper, the matching theorem supplies a specific example
of subsequent mathematical work enabled by reusable foundations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have formalized 28 of the 29 reviewed Davis--Kahan source results,
including the sine-theta theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators
and supported symmetric operator-ideal norms, and have refuted the
remaining printed assertion, Proposition~4.4.  The explicit
four-dimensional counterexample identifies the failure of a proposed
singular-value comparison in the direct-rotation argument and leads
to a corrected Q-norm conclusion.

The formalization also establishes a connected body of reusable
operator theory: principal angles and direct rotation, Borel spectral
calculus, approximation-number ideals, and domain-aware Sylvester
separation.  Portions were subsequently adapted into Tau Ceti.  The
real nuclear principal-plane matching theorem shows one further
mathematical direction developed using these foundations.  Together,
the source formalization, correction, and later theorem illustrate how
the formal development of classical mathematics can support further
research on the same objects.

\section{Reading the Lean signatures}
\label{app:lean-guide}
\label{app:reading-lean}

A Lean theorem declaration has the form
\[
  \texttt{theorem name (parameters) (hypotheses) : conclusion := proof}.
\]
Parameters introduce the mathematical objects under discussion.  A hypothesis
such as \texttt{(hA : IsSelfAdjoint A)} supplies the corresponding fact;
bundled predicates such as \texttt{IsTrialResidual} and
\texttt{IsExactSpectralDecomposition} collect related assumptions.  Braces mark
arguments normally inferred by Lean, while square brackets request typeclass
instances supplying ambient algebraic, analytic, or topological structure.  The proposition after the final colon is the theorem's conclusion
\citep{demoura2021lean4}.

In \Cref{lst:dk-sintheta-headline}, \texttt{RCLike} is the Mathlib abstraction
covering $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.  A type
\texttt{ContinuousLinearMap K F E} is a continuous linear map.  A type
\texttt{LinearPMap K E E} is a linear partial map whose
domain is a submodule; this is the representation used for possibly unbounded
operators.  \texttt{N.Mem T} states that the ideal gauge associated with $\sym{NormN}$ is
finite on $T$, and \texttt{N.gaugeReal T} is the corresponding real value.  The
suffix \texttt{Real} refers to the codomain of the gauge and not to the scalar
field.
