\begin{abstract}
We formalize the Davis--Kahan theory of spectral-subspace perturbations
from its 1970 primary source in Lean~4.  Of 29 reviewed result-level
statements, 28 are proved under their source hypotheses; the remaining
statement, Proposition~4.4, is refuted.  The development includes the
sine and tangent theorems, direct rotations, principal-angle operators,
Sylvester separation, unitarily invariant operator-ideal norms, and
unbounded self-adjoint operators.  We place these constructions in their
mathematical lineage, from the geometry of principal angles through
the theory of projections and the spectral methods underlying Davis
and Kahan's estimates.

The formalization required reusable operator theory, portions of which
were subsequently adapted into Tau Ceti, with additional foundations
adapted from Mathlib and the Spectra project.  Examining the full
displacement assertion in Proposition~4.4 produced a real
four-dimensional counterexample with arbitrarily small positive principal
angles.  We locate the failed singular-value comparison and prove a
corrected optimality statement for Q-norms.  The resulting infrastructure
also supports a later, separately proved theorem reducing nuclear-norm
optimal real subspace transport to weighted matching on principal
angles.  These outcomes show how developing the foundations of a
classical mathematical theory can support both a source correction and
new formal research.

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

When a self-adjoint operator is perturbed, an individual eigenvector may
be unstable even when the invariant subspace to which it belongs remains
well determined.  For instance, a multiple eigenvalue admits many
orthonormal eigenbases, while the span of the corresponding eigenvectors
is independent of that choice.  The Davis--Kahan theory quantifies the
stability of such subspaces through their principal angles, the
separation of relevant parts of the spectrum, and a perturbation or
computable residual \citep{davis1970rotation}.  This point of view
connects infinite-dimensional operator theory with the numerical
analysis of approximate eigenvectors.

The 1970 Davis--Kahan paper is an unusually rich formalization target.
It unifies the geometry of two closed subspaces with spectral
perturbation estimates, and frequently states conclusions for real and
complex Hilbert spaces, possibly unbounded self-adjoint operators, and
unitarily invariant norms on operator ideals.  A finite-matrix
specialization captures familiar sine-theta inequalities but omits
substantial parts of the source: domain conditions, half-infinite
spectral-gap configurations, and the behavior of operator ideals in
infinite dimensions.  The historical development of these mathematical
tools is traced in \cref{sec:related}.

Our Lean~4 formalization covers a reviewed set of 29 result-level
statements from the 1970 paper.  We prove 28 at their reviewed source
scope, including a sine-theta estimate for unbounded self-adjoint
operators and supported symmetric operator-ideal norms.  The remaining
statement, Proposition~4.4, asserts a false full-displacement
optimality property for direct rotation; a parameterized real
four-dimensional counterexample refutes it.  The complete source
inventory appears in Appendix~\ref{app:dk-inventory}.

To establish these statements, we developed and reused mathematical
interfaces for principal-angle operators, direct rotation, approximation
numbers, symmetric ideals, Borel spectral calculus, and domain-aware
Sylvester estimates.  Some foundations developed here were later
adapted into \href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau Ceti};
other parts were adapted from existing Mathlib and Spectra developments.
The formalization and its upstream contributions constitute a reusable
base for subsequent theorems.  In \cref{sec:example-transport} we give
one concrete example: the exact global minimum of nuclear displacement
under real orthogonal subspace transport is characterized by weighted
matching of principal angles.  This follow-on question arose from the
Proposition~4.4 investigation and uses the foundations developed during
the formalization effort.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A source-level formalization.} The 29-result review of
Davis--Kahan (1970) comprises 28 proved results and a formally refuted
Proposition~4.4.  The sine-theta theorem retains the real/complex
setting, unbounded self-adjoint domains, ordered as well as
interval/exterior gaps, and where-defined operator-ideal norms
(\cref{sec:coverage,sec:sine-theta}).
\item \textbf{An operator-theoretic library.} The proof develops
mathematical interfaces spanning the historical foundations of the
paper.  Parts of these interfaces were subsequently adapted into Tau
Ceti, with documented distinctions between adapted and independently
developed upstream implementations (\cref{sec:related,sec:foundations}).
\item \textbf{A correction to the 1970 source.} We give a real
four-dimensional counterexample at arbitrarily small positive angles,
identify the invalid Ky Fan comparison in Proposition~4.4, and prove a
valid Q-norm consequence of Proposition~4.3 (\cref{sec:prop44}).
\item \textbf{A formalization-enabled extension.} We exhibit a proved
reduction from unrestricted nuclear-optimal real transport to weighted
principal-plane matching (\cref{sec:example-transport}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Historical setting and mathematical foundations}
\label{sec:related}

The main components of Davis--Kahan theory originated in several
mathematical traditions.  Principal angles describe how subspaces meet;
orthogonal projections and spectral measures make the geometry
available in Hilbert space; Sylvester estimates turn spectral separation
into a bound; and symmetric operator norms determine which displacement
measure is controlled.  Their combination in the 1970 paper explains
both the scope of our formalization and the source error found in its
direct-rotation section.

\subsection{From Jordan's principal angles to pairs of projections}
\label{sec:related-classical}

Camille Jordan's 1875 study of higher-dimensional geometry contains the
early theory of principal angles between linear subspaces
\citep{jordan1875essai}.  In finite dimensions, let $E,F$ be subspaces
with orthonormal coordinate matrices $X,Y$.  The singular values of
$X^*Y$ are the cosines of their principal angles:
\[
  \sigma_j(X^*Y)=\cos\theta_j,\qquad 0\leq\theta_j\leq\frac{\pi}{2}.
\]
Equivalently, if $P$ and $Q$ project orthogonally onto $E$ and $F$,
then the compression $PQP$ on $E$ has eigenvalues
$\cos^2\theta_j$.  These formulations express the relative position of
the subspaces without privileging a particular choice of basis.  The
principal-angle viewpoint was later developed into numerical procedures
for computing the angles and their associated vectors by Bj\"orck and
Golub \citep{bjorck1973numerical}.

The passage to infinite-dimensional Hilbert space changes the
representation: orthogonal projections remain bounded, but an angle
operator may have continuous spectrum.  Jacques Dixmier studied the
relative positions of closed subspaces in 1948
\citep{dixmier1948position}.  Chandler Davis's 1958 \emph{Separation
of Two Linear Subspaces} introduced closeness and separation operators
that extend the cosine/sine viewpoint to Hilbert space and organized
extremal questions around them \citep{davis1958separation}.  Paul
Halmos's 1969 \emph{Two Subspaces} described a general pair by its
intersection and orthogonality components together with a generic
part modeled by an operator graph \citep{halmos1969two}.  These
constructions give a common language for both the finite principal
planes of the counterexample and the operator-valued angles needed for
the full perturbation theory.

\subsection{Nearby projections and the canonical rotation}
\label{sec:related-projections}

A different question asks for an ambient unitary that actually carries
one closed subspace to another.  B\'ela Sz.-Nagy proved in the 1940s
that orthogonal projections $P,Q$ satisfying $\|P-Q\|<1$ are related by
a unitary conjugation, in work motivated by the perturbation of
self-adjoint operators \citep{sznagy1947perturbations,simon2018kato}.
Tosio Kato developed an explicit algebraic construction and used it in
his perturbation theory \citep{kato1966perturbation,simon2018kato}.
The familiar intertwiner
\[
  T=QP+(I-Q)(I-P),\qquad TP=QT,
\]
becomes invertible under the small-gap condition.  Its unitary polar
factor transports the projections:
\[
  U=T(T^*T)^{-1/2},\qquad UPU^*=Q.
\]
For nearby orthogonal projections this is the canonical direct
rotation.  The formula also exhibits why the geometry calls for polar
decomposition and functional calculus, rather than just a choice of
orthonormal bases.  The finite principal-plane rotations and the
operator construction describe the same transformation from different
points of view.

Davis and Kahan's direct rotation refines this transport question with
symmetries, commutation properties, and extremal displacement results
\citep{davis1970rotation}.  The acute condition determines a canonical
branch.  Outside that regime, subspaces can contain orthogonal defect
directions and transport requires additional compatibility conditions.
Those cases are represented explicitly in the source formalization,
including when finite-dimensional principal-angle lists are no longer
available.

\subsection{Spectral projections, unbounded operators, and the residual}
\label{sec:related-spectral}

The spectral theorem for self-adjoint operators provides the analytic
counterpart to the geometry.  A self-adjoint operator determines
spectral projections and a functional calculus; for an unbounded
operator, its domain is part of the mathematical object.  Classical
perturbation theory developed these tools to follow spectral clusters
as an operator varies, and Kato's 1966 monograph provides a systematic
account \citep{kato1966perturbation,simon2018kato}.  This distinction
matters when an eigenvalue cluster is separated from an unbounded
portion of the spectrum: the associated spectral projection is bounded
even though the underlying self-adjoint operator need not be.

Davis investigated the rotation of eigenspaces under perturbation in
his 1963 and 1965 papers \citep{davis1963rotation,davis1965rotation}.
The 1970 collaboration with William Kahan brought that development
together with computable error bounds for approximate invariant
subspaces \citep{davis1970rotation}.  One may know a trial-coordinate
operator $A_0$ and an isometry $E_0$ into the ambient space without
knowing an exact eigenbasis.  The residual
\[
  R=AE_0-E_0A_0
\]
measures the failure of the trial space to be invariant under the
ambient operator.  A spectral gap $\delta$ separating the target block
from its complement turns this algebraic defect into a geometric
bound, schematically
\[
  \delta\,\|\sin\Theta\|\leq\|R\|.
\]
The sine operator describes the component of the trial subspace
orthogonal to the desired exact spectral subspace.  The resulting
statement is basis-independent: it bounds subspace movement rather
than the arbitrary rotation of eigenvectors within a degenerate
spectral cluster.

\subsection{Sylvester separation as the analytic bridge}
\label{sec:related-sylvester}

The operator equation $BX-XA=Y$ belongs to a line of research beginning
with Sylvester's matrix equations in 1884
\citep{sylvester1884equation}.  Rosenblum studied spectral separation
for operator equations in 1956 and later addressed possibly unbounded
self-adjoint coefficients \citep{rosenblum1956equation,rosenblum1969selfadjoint}.
Davis and Kahan use the Sylvester map to connect the residual to the
angle operator.  If $F_1$ is an isometric coordinate map for the
complementary spectral subspace, then $X=F_1^*E_0$ satisfies, with the
appropriate domains,
\[
  \Lambda_1 X-XA_0=F_1^*R.
\]
An interval/exterior or ordered spectral separation then yields the
factor $\delta$ in the sine estimate.  This is why the formalization
must represent operator domains and not merely an everywhere-defined
matrix product.  The domain-aware Sylvester interface in
\cref{sec:foundations} is a direct implementation of this link between
the classical operator equation and the perturbation theorem.

\subsection{Singular values, symmetric norms, and the displacement problem}
\label{sec:related-norms}

The norm used to measure rotation is itself a separate mathematical
choice.  John von Neumann characterized unitarily invariant matrix
norms using symmetric gauge functions of singular values in 1937
\citep{vonneumann1937matrix}.  Robert Schatten developed the
corresponding operator-ideal perspective
\citep{schatten1950crossspaces}; Ky Fan's inequalities provide the
partial-sum comparisons that control entire classes of such norms
\citep{fan1951maximum,bhatia1997matrix}.  For example, the nuclear
norm sums singular values, whereas the Frobenius norm takes the
Euclidean norm of their vector.  They can therefore order two
transporter displacements differently.

Davis had already proved Hilbert--Schmidt and finite-dimensional
Schatten-$p$ extremal properties for $p\geq2$ in 1958
\citep[Theorems~7.3--7.4]{davis1958separation}.  Davis and Kahan later
showed that direct rotation minimizes certain restricted and squared
displacement costs, but also exhibited a complex-phase example in
which full nuclear displacement is smaller for another transporter
\citep[Example~4.2 and Proposition~4.3]{davis1970rotation}.  Their
Proposition~4.4 asserts full-displacement optimality in the real case
when all principal angles are at most $\pi/3$.  The real
four-dimensional family in \cref{sec:prop44} violates that conclusion:
its competing transporter concentrates displacement into fewer
singular directions, lowering their sum.

Bolotnikov, Li, and Rodman later studied the general minimum-distortion
problem for unitary maps constrained to transport subspaces, including
complete Frobenius results and a counterexample for Schatten exponents
below two \citep{bolotnikov2001minimal}.  Qiu, Zhang, and Li examined
unitarily invariant Grassmann-space metrics
\citep{qiu2006grassmann}; Zhang and Qiu investigated canonical-angle
inequalities and trace-norm nonminimality
\citep{zhang2010canonicalangles}.  This sequence situates the
Proposition~4.4 correction in a continuing study of how a
subspace-invariant geometry interacts with different spectral costs.

\subsection{Formal libraries and the ancestry of the development}
\label{sec:related-formal}

The formalization is built on Lean~4 and Mathlib
\citep{demoura2021lean4,mathlib2020,dedecker2025cfc}.
Adam Bornemann's \emph{Spectra} project supplies earlier formal
foundations for projection-valued measures and Borel spectral calculus,
parts of which were adapted and generalized in this project
\citep{bornemann2026spectra}.  The additional formal mathematics
includes partial-map representations for unbounded operators, spectral
cutoffs and approximations, principal-angle and direct-rotation
interfaces, and symmetric operator ideals.

Several results developed while formalizing Davis--Kahan were later
adapted into the \href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau
Ceti Lean library} as part of its operator-theory roadmap.  For
example, the upstream
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/13599}{Courant--Fischer
and Weyl contribution} and
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/14241}{rectangular
unitarily invariant seminorm contribution} document DKPS donor
adaptations.  The
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti/pull/17527}{projection-valued
measure contribution} incorporates work with Spectra ancestry.  Other
roadmap items, including canonical polar decomposition, were
implemented independently by upstream contributors.  The repository's
contribution census preserves these distinctions between a classical
mathematical source, a formalization donor, and an independently
implemented upstream theorem.

\paragraph{How the historical ingredients enter the formal proof.}
The principal-angle and projection literature supplies the geometric
operators; the Sz.-Nagy--Kato transport construction supplies the
canonical unitary; spectral calculus supplies the exact spectral
subspaces; the Sylvester equation connects them to a residual; and
symmetric ideals measure the resulting angle and perturbation
operators.  \Cref{sec:coverage,sec:foundations} follows this dependency
chain through the formal development, while \cref{sec:prop44} shows how
its norm-comparison layer exposed a false step in the original paper.

\section{Source coverage and the sine-theta theorem}
\label{sec:coverage}

The formalization covers \DKResultCount{} result-level statements in Davis
and Kahan's paper: the Section~2 sine and tangent inequalities, the geometry
and extremal properties of direct rotations, the bounded and unbounded
Sylvester estimates, the generalized Section~6 perturbation bounds, and the
spectral branch-selection theorems. \DKProvedCount{} have checked Lean proofs
with their reviewed source hypotheses; Proposition~4.4 is refuted as printed.
Appendix~\ref{app:dk-inventory} specifies the counting rule and gives the
complete result table, and Appendix~\ref{app:source-review} states the
comparison procedure.

Appendix~\ref{app:notation} collects the symbols used in the sine-theta
and direct-rotation arguments.

These results depend on spectral and operator-ideal constructions that cannot
be reduced to finite matrices without changing the assumptions of the source
statements.

\subsection{The full-scope sine-theta theorem}
\label{sec:sine-theta}

\subsubsection{Trial and exact subspaces}

Davis and Kahan distinguish a reference operator $\sym{A}$ from a perturbed operator
$\sym{A}+\sym{H}$.  For the formal statement we write $\sym{Acurrent}$ for the perturbed full-space
operator, corresponding to the source's $\sym{A}+\sym{H}$.

Let $\sym{Eambient}$ be the ambient Hilbert space.  Let $\sym{A0}$ be a self-adjoint operator on a
trial-coordinate space $\sym{Ftrial}$, and let
\[
  \sym{E0}:\sym{Ftrial}\to \sym{Eambient}
\]
be an isometric coordinate map for the trial subspace.  The residual compares
applying the ambient operator after embedding with applying the trial operator
before embedding:
\begin{equation}
  \sym{R} = \sym{Acurrent}\sym{E0}-\sym{E0}\sym{A0}.
  \label{eq:residual}
\end{equation}
When the operators are unbounded, \cref{eq:residual} is asserted on
$\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$ and $\sym{R}$ is the bounded residual that agrees there.
The Lean predicate \texttt{IsTrialResidual} packages the isometry, the required
domain inclusion, and this residual identity.

Let $\sym{F0}:\sym{Hexact}\to \sym{Eambient}$ be isometric coordinates for the desired exact spectral
subspace and let $\sym{F1}:\sym{Gunwanted}\to \sym{Eambient}$ be isometric coordinates for its orthogonal
complement.  A self-adjoint operator $\sym{Lambda1}$ on $\sym{Gunwanted}$ describes the
complementary spectral block through
\[
  \sym{Acurrent}\sym{F1} = \sym{F1}\sym{Lambda1}
\]
on its domain.  The predicate \texttt{IsExactSpectralDecomposition} also records
orthogonality and completeness of the two coordinate ranges.

\subsubsection{The sine operator and its rectangular representative}

The source angle operator acts on the trial-coordinate space.  If
$\sym{Pexact}=\sym{F0}\like{F0}{F_0^*}$ is the orthogonal projection onto the desired exact subspace, then
\[
  \cos\sym{Theta0} = (\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0})^{1/2},
  \qquad
  \sym{sinTheta0} = (I-\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0})^{1/2}.
\]
The analytic proof uses the rectangular complementary block
\begin{equation}
  \sym{S}=(I-\sym{Pexact})\sym{E0}.
  \label{eq:rectangular-sine}
\end{equation}
The source-facing Lean theorem is stated on a positive operator on the
trial-coordinate space.  The rectangular block has codomain $\sym{Eambient}$, while the
positive sine has codomain $\sym{Ftrial}$.  Their relationship is
\[
  \sym{S}^*\sym{S}
  = \like{E0}{E_0^*}(I-\sym{Pexact})\sym{E0}
  = I-\like{E0}{E_0^*}\sym{Pexact}\sym{E0},
\]
so
\begin{equation}
  |\sym{S}|=(\sym{S}^*\sym{S})^{1/2}=\sym{sinTheta0}.
  \label{eq:modulus-sine}
\end{equation}
The Lean API constructs the directed angle on trial coordinates by functional
calculus and defines its sine by applying $\sin$ through the same calculus.
Under the isometry hypotheses, Lean proves that this operator is exactly the
modulus $|\sym{S}|$ in \cref{eq:modulus-sine}.  A polar decomposition $\sym{S}=\sym{PolarU}|\sym{S}|$ and
the ideal laws then imply
\[
  \sym{NormN}(\sym{S})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})
\]
whenever the norm is defined.  The rectangular theorem is the analytic proof
engine; the selected source-facing theorem transports its conclusion to the
positive sine operator.

\subsubsection{Gap geometry and the inequality}

Fix $\sym{delta}>0$. The separation hypothesis has the following alternatives.
For some $\sym{betaEndpoint}\leq\sym{alphaEndpoint}$, one of the two spectra
$\sigma(\sym{A0})$ and $\sigma(\sym{Lambda1})$ lies in $[\sym{betaEndpoint},\sym{alphaEndpoint}]$ and the other
lies in
\[
 (-\infty,\sym{betaEndpoint}-\sym{delta}]\;\cup\;[\sym{alphaEndpoint}+\sym{delta},\infty).
\]
Alternatively, for some real $b$, the operator forms satisfy either
\[
 \sym{A0}\geq (b+\sym{delta})I,\quad \sym{Lambda1}\leq bI,
 \qquad\text{or}\qquad
 \sym{A0}\leq bI,\quad \sym{Lambda1}\geq (b+\sym{delta})I.
\]
Each form inequality is asserted on the domain of the corresponding operator;
for example, $\sym{A0}\geq aI$ means
$\langle \sym{A0}x,x\rangle\geq a\|x\|^2$ for every
$x\in\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$. For self-adjoint operators the source's ordered
spectral half-line hypotheses imply these form bounds. The Lean predicate
\texttt{FormBoundedSylvesterGap} records precisely the finite-interval
alternative, in either spectral order, and these two ordered form alternatives.

Both blocks may be unbounded in an ordered configuration. For instance,
on $\ell^2(\mathbb N_{\geq1})$, take diagonal operators
$\sym{A0}e_n=(n+\sym{delta})e_n$ and $\sym{Lambda1}e_n=-ne_n$, on the domains where their
images are square summable. They satisfy the first ordered configuration
with $b=0$, while neither spectrum lies in a finite interval. This example
explains why retaining the half-infinite alternatives changes the theorem's
applicability.

The source also writes a common-domain equality for the unbounded trial
configuration.  That equality discharges the forward domain condition consumed
by \texttt{IsTrialResidual}.  Source correspondence is checked from the combined
hypotheses, with this equality supplying the required domain condition.

Appendix~\ref{app:lean-guide} explains the general Lean syntax in the
following theorem signature; the geometric and spectral hypotheses have been
defined above.

\begin{theorem}[Sine-theta estimate]
\label{thm:sine-theta}
Let the scalar field be $\mathbb R$ or $\mathbb C$, let $\sym{Eambient}$ be separable,
and let $\sym{Acurrent}$, $\sym{A0}$, and $\sym{Lambda1}$ be self-adjoint operators on the
Hilbert spaces above. Suppose the isometries, domain inclusions, residual
identity, exact complementary decomposition, and one of the three gap
configurations hold. Let $\sym{NormN}$ be a supported normalized symmetric
operator-ideal norm. Whenever $\sym{sinTheta0}$ and $\sym{R}$ belong to its domain,
\begin{equation}
 \sym{delta}\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R}).
 \label{eq:dk-sine}
\end{equation}
\end{theorem}

    (F0 : ContinuousLinearMap K H E)
    (F1 : ContinuousLinearMap K G E)
    (R : ContinuousLinearMap K F E)
    (hA : IsSelfAdjoint A)
    (hA0 : IsSelfAdjoint A0)
    (hLambda1 : IsSelfAdjoint Lambda1)
    (htrial : IsTrialResidual A A0 E0 R)
    (hexact : IsExactSpectralDecomposition A Lambda1 F0 F1)
    (delta : Real) (hdelta : 0 < delta)
    (hgap : FormBoundedSylvesterGap A0 Lambda1 delta) :
    N.Mem (sourceDirectedSinThetaOperator E0 F0) ->
    N.Mem R ->
      delta * N.gaugeReal (sourceDirectedSinThetaOperator E0 F0) <=
        N.gaugeReal R
\end{lstlisting}

The two ideal-membership hypotheses express the ``where-defined'' restriction.
The proof first estimates the rectangular map $\sym{S}=(I-\sym{F0}\like{F0}{F_0^*})\sym{E0}$ and then
uses \cref{eq:modulus-sine} and modulus invariance of the ideal gauge. The
ambient Hilbert-space instances are omitted from the displayed Lean signature.
The theorem contains all three separation configurations encoded by its gap
hypothesis.  The ordered half-infinite cases have distinct hypotheses from
the finite interval/exterior specialization, so source correspondence is checked
against the complete family of gap hypotheses.

\subsubsection{Why the Sylvester estimate gives the angle bound}

Set $\sym{X}=\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{E0}$. The exact complementary decomposition gives
$\sym{S}=\sym{F1}\sym{X}$, and multiplication by the isometry $\sym{F1}$ preserves the supported
singular-value gauges. Self-adjointness, the domain hypotheses, and the
residual identity yield the Sylvester relation
\[
 \sym{Lambda1} \sym{X}-\sym{X}\sym{A0}=\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{R}
\]
on $\operatorname{dom}(\sym{A0})$, with the left side represented by the bounded
right side. The separated Sylvester estimate gives
\[
 \sym{delta} \sym{NormN}(\sym{X})\leq \sym{NormN}(\like{F1}{F_1^*}\sym{R})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R}).
\]
Combining this with $\sym{NormN}(\sym{X})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{S})=\sym{NormN}(\sym{sinTheta0})$ proves the conclusion.
For unbounded operators, deriving the domain compatibility for $\sym{X}$ is part of
the argument; the displayed relation is not an everywhere-defined composition
of unbounded maps. The constant-one estimate uses the specified interval or
ordered gap geometry, rather than an arbitrary assertion that the two spectra
are disjoint.


The rank-one equality example in \cref{fig:sine-theta-rank-one} takes
$\sym{Acurrent}=\operatorname{diag}(0,\sym{delta})$, $\sym{A0}=0$, and the trial unit vector
$\like{v}{v_\theta}=(\cos\sym{theta},\sin\sym{theta})$.  Then the complementary component
has length $\sin\sym{theta}$ and the residual has length $\sym{delta}\sin\sym{theta}$.
This geometric picture is a finite-dimensional specialization of the
preceding theorem, whose domain and operator-ideal hypotheses remain as
stated above.

\section{Reusable operator-theoretic foundations}
\label{sec:foundations}
\label{sec:dk-rest}

The proofs draw on three closely related areas: the geometry of two
subspaces, symmetric operator ideals and majorization, and Sylvester
estimates for spectrally separated operators.

\subsection{Direct rotations and the geometry of two subspaces}

Let $\sym{Psource}$ and $\sym{Qtarget}$ be the orthogonal projections onto closed subspaces $\sym{Usource}$ and
$\sym{Vtarget}$.  Davis and Kahan organize their geometry through the operators obtained
from $\sym{Psource}$, $\sym{Qtarget}$, and their complements.  In the acute case, the compression
$\sym{Psource}\sym{Qtarget}\sym{Psource}$ has no kernel on $\sym{Usource}$ and determines the cosine of the angle operator;
the complementary compression determines its sine.  Polar decomposition then
supplies the partial isometries that pair the corresponding principal
subspaces.  Combining these pieces gives the direct rotation, an orthogonal or
unitary operator $\sym{Rrotation}$ satisfying $\sym{Rrotation}(\sym{Usource})=\sym{Vtarget}$ and rotating along the principal
planes.

This construction explains several results that look separate when stated only
at theorem level.  Existence and uniqueness in the acute case, the
principal-square-root characterization, commutation with the angle operators,
and reversal symmetry all express compatibility among the same projection and
polar-decomposition data.  The nonacute theory records the additional kernel
conditions needed when principal angles reach $\pi/2$.  For compact angle
operators, the resulting decomposition reduces to the familiar sequence of
principal angles; the general Hilbert-space statements do not require such a
discrete spectrum.

Section~8 returns to this geometry from the spectral side.  Perturbation
smallness and spectral repulsion rule out the wrong branch and place the two
spectral subspaces in the acute regime where the direct rotation is available.
In the formal development this branch-selection argument uses the same
unbounded spectral calculus as the perturbation theorems.  Acuteness therefore
follows from the spectral hypotheses, with no separate matrix-level assumption.

\subsection{Approximation numbers, majorization, and unitarily invariant norms}

Davis and Kahan formulate many extremal statements simultaneously for broad
classes of unitarily invariant norms.  In finite dimensions such a norm can be
described by a symmetric gauge function of the singular values.  The
infinite-dimensional formalization uses approximation numbers as the singular
value sequence of a compact operator and symmetric norming functions to encode
the gauge.  Ky Fan partial sums then provide the bridge from pointwise or
majorization statements to norm inequalities.

This layer is essential in the extremal displacement argument. If $\sym{Rrotation}$ is the direct rotation and $\sym{Wcompetitor}$
is any unitary carrying $\sym{Usource}$ onto $\sym{Vtarget}$, the restricted displacement has
singular-value extremality strong enough to imply
unitarily invariant norm minimality.  Davis and Kahan also prove a
majorization theorem for the squared full displacement
$(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})^*(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})$.  Squaring changes the relevant singular values from
$s_j(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})$ to $s_j(I-\sym{Wcompetitor})^2$, so a majorization statement at the squared level
does not automatically imply the corresponding statement for the unsquared
trace norm.  Proposition~4.4 attempts precisely that stronger passage. The
counterexample in Section~\ref{sec:prop44} shows where it fails; the
$\sym{NormQ}$-norm assertion and quantitative bound in
Section~\ref{sec:prop44} give two valid conclusions.

The same operator-ideal machinery is used in the perturbation inequalities.
For the sine theorem, the statement $\sym{delta}\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{S})\leq \sym{NormN}(\sym{R})$ is meaningful for
any supported symmetric operator ideal once both $S$ and the residual belong to
that ideal.  The formal theorem therefore keeps the source's ``where-defined''
convention and does not build an accidental finite-dimensional compactness
assumption into the statement.

\subsection{Sylvester separation and unbounded operators}

The analytic engine behind the sine and tangent estimates is the Sylvester map
\[
  \sym{X} \longmapsto AX-XB.
\]
Under the interval/exterior or ordered spectral-gap configurations used above,
Davis and Kahan obtain lower bounds of the form
\[
  \delta\,\sym{NormN}(\sym{X}) \leq \sym{NormN}(AX-XB).
\]
The perturbation theorems result by identifying the appropriate off-diagonal or
trial-subspace operator $\sym{X}$ and rewriting its Sylvester residual in terms of the
original perturbation.

The unbounded case forces domain information into this argument.  Expressions
such as $AX-XB$ are only meaningful on the appropriate domains, and the
spectral separation may be encoded by a finite interval/exterior configuration
or by an ordered pair of half-lines.  The reusable development represents
unbounded operators as domain-carrying linear partial maps, develops the
self-adjoint and semibounded interfaces needed by the proof, and connects
spectral hypotheses to a form-bounded Sylvester gap.  Strong spectral cutoffs
reduce parts of the argument to bounded operators; convergence of the relevant
approximation numbers then permits passage back to the unbounded setting.

Section~6 reuses this engine after weakening the trial-coordinate assumptions.
Reflection pinching and direct-sum norm comparisons control the resulting block
operators, while the Sylvester estimate supplies the spectral-gap factor.  This
produces the generalized sine and tangent theorems and the pairwise-gap
square-norm estimate at the scope stated in the source.

\subsection{Formal foundations and ancestry}

The analytic and geometric ingredients above required interfaces not
previously available in sufficient generality in the project's pinned
Lean dependencies.  The development adds the principal-angle and
projection constructions, approximation numbers and symmetric
operator ideals, polar decomposition and direct rotations, Borel
spectral calculus, unbounded self-adjoint partial maps, and real/complex
transfer.  Complexification allows spectral arguments to be developed
using complex functional calculus and then specialized to real Hilbert
spaces when the historical statement covers both fields.

Mathlib provides the basic algebraic, topological, Hilbert-space, and
continuous-functional-calculus framework
\citep{mathlib2020,dedecker2025cfc}.  Projection-valued-measure and
Borel-calculus constructions also use ideas adapted from Bornemann's
Spectra development \citep{bornemann2026spectra}.  Several DKPS donor
results were subsequently integrated into the
\href{https://github.com/TauCetiProject/TauCeti}{Tau Ceti source
library}; other operator-theory roadmap results were proved
independently upstream.  The source-level statements and the
Proposition~4.4 counterexample remain in the Davis--Kahan package.
The mathematical ancestry of these interfaces is described in
\cref{sec:related-formal}.

\section{Discussion: what the formalization made accessible}
\label{sec:discussion}

The formalization brings together the geometric, spectral, and
norm-theoretic components of the 1970 paper in one reusable setting.
Its sine-theta statement includes ordered half-infinite gap cases and
unbounded self-adjoint domains; its direct-rotation results treat the
operator geometry of closed Hilbert subspaces; and its operator-ideal
layer supports norm comparisons beyond a single finite-dimensional
matrix norm.  These choices matter mathematically because changing
the scalar field, restricting to finite matrices, or suppressing the
domain of an unbounded operator changes which theorem has been proved.

Proposition~4.4 illustrates a different benefit of source-level
formalization.  Proposition~4.3 controls a squared-displacement
quantity, but a sum-of-singular-values inequality for the unsquared
displacement requires an additional argument.  The explicit
counterexample demonstrates that this further implication fails.  In
the positive direction, the Q-norm result identifies a class of costs
for which the squared-displacement theorem does establish direct-
rotation optimality.  The distinction is visible in the singular
values of the real four-dimensional example, rather than only in the
outcome of a proof search.

The new real nuclear matching theorem in \cref{sec:example-transport}
uses the same principal-angle, real/complex, and operator-norm
infrastructure.  It is a further result of the formalization research
program: the original source audit made the transport question
concrete, and the resulting library supported its formal investigation.
The separate spectral-transport manuscript develops the stronger
classification and infinite-dimensional results.  For the
formalization paper, the matching theorem supplies a specific example
of subsequent mathematical work enabled by reusable foundations.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have formalized 28 of the 29 reviewed Davis--Kahan source results,
including the sine-theta theorem for unbounded self-adjoint operators
and supported symmetric operator-ideal norms, and have refuted the
remaining printed assertion, Proposition~4.4.  The explicit
four-dimensional counterexample identifies the failure of a proposed
singular-value comparison in the direct-rotation argument and leads
to a corrected Q-norm conclusion.

The formalization also establishes a connected body of reusable
operator theory: principal angles and direct rotation, Borel spectral
calculus, approximation-number ideals, and domain-aware Sylvester
separation.  Portions were subsequently adapted into Tau Ceti.  The
real nuclear principal-plane matching theorem shows one further
mathematical direction developed using these foundations.  Together,
the source formalization, correction, and later theorem illustrate how
the formal development of classical mathematics can support further
research on the same objects.

\section{Reading the Lean signatures}
\label{app:lean-guide}
\label{app:reading-lean}

A Lean theorem declaration has the form
\[
  \texttt{theorem name (parameters) (hypotheses) : conclusion := proof}.
\]
Parameters introduce the mathematical objects under discussion.  A hypothesis
such as \texttt{(hA : IsSelfAdjoint A)} supplies the corresponding fact;
bundled predicates such as \texttt{IsTrialResidual} and
\texttt{IsExactSpectralDecomposition} collect related assumptions.  Braces mark
arguments normally inferred by Lean, while square brackets request typeclass
instances supplying ambient algebraic, analytic, or topological structure.  The proposition after the final colon is the theorem's conclusion
\citep{demoura2021lean4}.

In \Cref{lst:dk-sintheta-headline}, \texttt{RCLike} is the Mathlib abstraction
covering $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$.  A type
\texttt{ContinuousLinearMap K F E} is a continuous linear map.  A type
\texttt{LinearPMap K E E} is a linear partial map whose
domain is a submodule; this is the representation used for possibly unbounded
operators.  \texttt{N.Mem T} states that the ideal gauge associated with $\sym{NormN}$ is
finite on $T$, and \texttt{N.gaugeReal T} is the corresponding real value.  The
suffix \texttt{Real} refers to the codomain of the gauge and not to the scalar
field.
