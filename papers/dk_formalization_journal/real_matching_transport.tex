\section{Real transport as a weighted matching problem}
\label{sec:real-matching-transport}

The real four-dimensional formula determines the global minimum in every
finite real dimension for a class of spectral penalties. The reduction
uses the stationary operator's spectral blocks and a real orthogonal
normal form for a complex Hermitian projection.

Let $\rho:[0,2]\to\mathbb R$ be continuously differentiable, with
$\rho(0)=\rho'(0)=0$ and $\rho'(s)>0$ for $s>0$. Set
\[
 \phi(t)=\rho\bigl(2\sin(t/2)\bigr),\qquad 0\leq t\leq\pi.
\]
In dimension $n$, write
$\mathcal F_\rho(W)=\sum_{j=1}^n\rho(\sigma_j(I-W))$.
Assume that for some $c\in(0,\pi)$ the derivative $\phi'$ is strictly
increasing on $[0,c]$ and strictly decreasing on $[c,\pi]$.
These hypotheses include the Schatten powers $\rho(s)=s^p$, $p>1$,
and the Charbonnier penalty. Define the one-angle and two-angle costs
\[
 s_\rho(\theta)=\min\{2\phi(\theta),\rho(2)\},
\]
\[
 e_\rho(\theta,\psi)=
   2\min_{|\theta-\psi|/2\leq\delta\leq(\theta+\psi)/2}
    \left[\phi\left(\frac{\theta+\psi}{2}+\delta\right)
          +\phi\left(\frac{\theta+\psi}{2}-\delta\right)\right].
\]
A matching $M$ on $\{1,\ldots,k\}$ is a set of disjoint unordered
pairs; its unmatched vertices are denoted by $U(M)$.

\begin{theorem}[The exact real matching formula]
\label{thm:real-matching-minimum}
Let $P,Q$ be equal-rank real orthogonal projections on $\mathbb R^n$,
and let $\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k$ be their positive principal angles,
including multiplicities and right angles. Then
\begin{equation}
 \min_{\substack{W\in O(n)\\WPW^T=Q}}
   \sum_{j=1}^n\rho(\sigma_j(I-W))
 =\min_M\left\{
    \sum_{\{i,j\}\in M}e_\rho(\theta_i,\theta_j)
     +\sum_{i\in U(M)}s_\rho(\theta_i)\right\}.
 \label{eq:real-matching-minimum}
\end{equation}
An attaining transport acts on one principal plane for each unmatched
angle and couples the two principal planes of each matched pair.
It fixes the remaining common summands.
\end{theorem}

We first give the algebraic decomposition used in the proof.

\begin{lemma}[A Hermitian projection under real orthogonal coordinates]
\label{lem:hermitian-projection-real-blocks}
Every complex Hermitian projection is conjugate by a real orthogonal
matrix to a direct sum of scalar blocks $0,1$ and blocks
\[
 \begin{pmatrix}
   \lambda&-i\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}\\
   i\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}&1-\lambda
 \end{pmatrix},\qquad 0<\lambda\leq\tfrac12.
\]
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Write the projection as $A=X+iY$, where $X$ is real symmetric and
$Y$ is real skew-symmetric. The identities $A^2=A$ give
\[
 X^2-Y^2=X,\qquad XY+YX=Y.
\]
For real $v$, positivity and contractivity of $A$ give
$0\leq v^TXv\leq\|v\|^2$, so the eigenvalues of $X$ lie in $[0,1]$.
The second identity makes $Y$ map the $\lambda$-eigenspace of $X$
to the $(1-\lambda)$-eigenspace. On the former space,
\[
 -Y^2=\lambda(1-\lambda)I.
\]
For $0<\lambda<1/2$, an orthonormal basis $v_j$ of the first space
is therefore matched with the orthonormal basis
$Yv_j/\sqrt{\lambda(1-\lambda)}$ of the second. Each pair gives
the displayed block. On the $1/2$-eigenspace, $2Y$ is an orthogonal
complex structure; choose successive orthonormal pairs $v,2Yv$.
On the zero and one eigenspaces, skew-symmetry and $Y^2=0$ force
$Y=0$, giving the scalar blocks.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of \cref{thm:real-matching-minimum}]
The feasible set is nonempty and compact. Choose a global minimizer $W$.
Extend $\phi$ evenly and periodically to the real line. Its extension is
continuously differentiable, including at zero and $\pi$.
Let $K$ be the real skew-symmetric spectral gradient of $W$: on a real
rotation block through $t$, its value is $\phi'(t)$ times the quarter-turn
matrix, and on the eigenvalues $1,-1$ it is zero.
It commutes with $W$.

For every real skew-symmetric $H$, the spectral first-variation formula
\citep{lewis1996derivatives} gives
\[
 \left.\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal F_\rho(W\exp(tH))\right|_{t=0}
       =-\operatorname{tr}(KH).
\]
As in the proof of \cref{eq:schatten-first-variation}, one may use a local
logarithm whose cut avoids the finite spectrum and apply the Hermitian
spectral derivative theorem. The periodic extension's continuous
differentiability covers the eigenvalues $1,-1$.
When $H$ commutes with $P$, the curve is feasible. Take $H$ to be the
block diagonal compression of $K$ to $P\oplus P^\perp$.
Then $-\operatorname{tr}(KH)=\|H\|_F^2$, so stationarity forces
both diagonal compressions to vanish. For $J=2P-I$ this says
\[
 JKJ=-K.
\]
Consequently $-K^2$ commutes with $P,W$, and $Q=WPW^T$.

Consider a positive spectral level $-K^2=g^2I$. The operator
$\mathcal I=K/g$ is an orthogonal complex structure, and $J$ is an
antilinear isometric involution for this complex structure. Its fixed
space is the range of $P$. Choosing a real orthonormal basis of that
fixed space identifies the whole level with its complexification,
with $J$ acting as conjugation. The restriction of $W$ is then a
complex unitary matrix $V$.

Every eigenphase of $V$ solves $\phi'(t)=g$ in $(0,\pi)$.
The single-peak hypothesis gives at most two roots, say $a,b$.
If there are two roots, write
\[
 V=e^{ia}I+(e^{ib}-e^{ia})A
\]
with a complex Hermitian projection $A$; if the roots coalesce,
$V$ is scalar. Apply \cref{lem:hermitian-projection-real-blocks}.
Its real change of coordinates preserves $J$ and therefore $P$.
It decomposes the real level into orthogonal $P,W,Q$-invariant
blocks of dimension two or four, with source ranks one or two.
The scalar-root case gives two-dimensional blocks directly.

On $\ker K$, the only eigenvalues of $W$ are $1,-1$, so $W$ is a
reflection. The real decomposition of two projections, applied to
$P$ and $(I-W)/2$, gives common invariant blocks of dimension at most
two. A one-dimensional block with $W=-1$ cannot occur at a global
minimum: replacing it by $+1$ preserves transport and lowers the cost.
The other one-dimensional blocks have zero cost and zero angle.

On a two-dimensional rank-one block, the cost is at least
$s_\rho(\theta)$. Indeed, its four orthogonal line transporters have
costs $2\phi(\theta)$, $2\phi(\pi-\theta)$, and $\rho(2)$ for
each of the two reflections; the second is no smaller than the first.
On a four-dimensional rank-two block, \cref{thm:real-four-dimensional-minimum}
gives the lower bound
\[
 \min\{e_\rho(\theta,\psi),
                 s_\rho(\theta)+s_\rho(\psi)\}.
\]
To see the equivalence with that theorem's formula, note that
$e_\rho\leq2\phi(\theta)+2\phi(\psi)$ and
$e_\rho\leq2\rho(2)$, the latter by choosing the endpoint phase.
For $\theta\geq\psi$, the remaining choice is exactly
$\rho(2)+2\phi(\psi)$.

Principal-angle multisets add across common reducing blocks of $P,Q$.
Selecting a pair or two singletons in each four-dimensional block
therefore gives a matching whose value is no larger than the cost of
$W$. Zero angles can be discarded: $s_\rho(0)=0$ and
$e_\rho(\theta,0)=2\phi(\theta)\geq s_\rho(\theta)$.
This proves the lower bound in \cref{eq:real-matching-minimum}.

Conversely, choose a cosine--sine decomposition of $P,Q$ and any matching.
Use an attaining common rotation from
\cref{thm:real-four-dimensional-minimum} on each matched pair of principal
planes. On each unmatched plane choose the cheaper canonical rotation
or reflection. Fix every remaining common direction. The resulting
orthogonal sum is feasible and has exactly the matching's value,
proving the reverse inequality and attainment.
\end{proof}

Put $g_{ij}=s_\rho(\theta_i)+s_\rho(\theta_j)-e_\rho(\theta_i,\theta_j)$.
Then \cref{eq:real-matching-minimum} is equivalently
\[
 \sum_i s_\rho(\theta_i)-\max_M\sum_{\{i,j\}\in M}g_{ij}.
\]
Edges of nonpositive weight may be omitted. Thus evaluation of the scalar
costs followed by a maximum-weight matching gives an exact global
optimization procedure. At $p=3/2$ and for the Charbonnier penalty the
edge costs are explicit; other Schatten exponents require only the
one-dimensional scalar balance equation.

The same matching solves nearest commuting-orthogonal correction. Given
$U\in O(n)$ and a prescribed projection $P$, put $Q=UPU^T$. The map
$W\mapsto V=W^TU$ is a bijection from transporters of $P$ onto $Q$
to orthogonal matrices commuting with $P$. Since
$U-V=(I-W^T)U$, its displacement has the same singular values as
$I-W$. Thus the formula computes an orthogonal correction that preserves
the prescribed subspace and has the least spectral penalty among all
such corrections.

\begin{corollary}[The exact real nuclear minimum]
\label{cor:real-nuclear-matching}
The matching formula also holds for $\rho(s)=s$, with
\[
 s_1(\theta)=\min\{4\sin(\theta/2),2\},\qquad
 e_1(\theta,\psi)=4\sin\frac{\theta+\psi}{2}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $s\in[0,2]$ the Charbonnier penalties satisfy
$|\rho_\tau(s)-s|\leq\tau$. Their objectives converge uniformly on
$O(n)$, so their constrained minima converge to the nuclear minimum.
The one-angle and two-angle costs converge uniformly as well.
There are finitely many matchings, so the minimum of their costs
converges to the minimum of the limiting costs. The limits are exactly
the two displayed expressions, by \cref{eq:real-four-nuclear} and its
one-angle counterpart.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[Two sorted sums for real nuclear transport]
\label{thm:real-nuclear-two-sums}
Sort the positive principal angles as
$\theta_1\geq\cdots\geq\theta_k>0$.
For a descending list $t=(t_1,\ldots,t_m)$, append one zero if $m$ is
odd, and define
\[
 B(t)=4\sum_{j=1}^{\lceil m/2\rceil}
           \sin\frac{t_{2j-1}+t_{2j}}2,
 \qquad B(\varnothing)=0.
\]
For $k\geq1$, the exact real nuclear transport minimum is
\begin{equation}
 \min_{WPW^T=Q}\|I-W\|_1
  =\min\left\{B(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k),\quad
               2+B(\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_k)\right\}.
 \label{eq:real-nuclear-two-sums}
\end{equation}
The first choice uses adjacent coupled principal planes and, when $k$ is
odd, a canonical rotation on the smallest-angle plane. The second first
reflects the largest-angle plane, then applies the same rule to the rest.
If $k=0$, the minimum is zero.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By \cref{cor:real-nuclear-matching}, a singleton angle $t$ can use a
canonical rotation of cost $4\sin(t/2)$ or a reflection of cost two;
a paired choice has cost $4\sin((t+u)/2)$.
There is an optimal matching with at most one singleton of each type.
Indeed, coupling two canonical singletons strictly lowers their cost,
and coupling two reflection singletons costs at most four.
Repeatedly making these replacements proves the assertion.

If a reflection singleton is present, it may be assigned to the largest
angle. If that angle is paired, exchange it with the reflection
singleton; the paired cost decreases because the smaller angle enters
the pair. If the largest angle is a canonical singleton, exchange the
two singleton types, which decreases the canonical cost.
Thus there is an optimum whose reflection singleton, if present, is
$\theta_1$.

A canonical singleton may be assigned to the smallest angle left.
For a fixed partner $a\geq0$, the function
\[
 u\longmapsto
 \sin\frac{a+u}{2}-\sin\frac u2
\]
is nonincreasing, since its derivative is
$[\cos((a+u)/2)-\cos(u/2)]/2\leq0$.
Hence exchanging a canonical singleton $u$ with a smaller paired angle
cannot increase the total cost. Repeating gives the asserted position.
Equivalently, represent this canonical singleton as a pair with a
single zero appended to the remaining angle list.

Finally, adjacent pairing minimizes the total paired cost in a sorted
even list. For $a\geq b\geq c\geq d\geq0$, put
$s=(a+b+c+d)/4$. Then
\[
 \sin\frac{a+b}{2}+\sin\frac{c+d}{2}
   =2\sin s\cos\frac{a+b-c-d}{4}.
\]
The two alternative pairings have the same factor $2\sin s\geq0$
and smaller absolute cosine arguments. Since all these arguments lie in
$[-\pi/2,\pi/2]$, neither alternative has smaller cost. If the two
largest entries are paired with different partners, this exchange pairs
them together without increasing cost. Induction proves adjacent pairing
for the full list, including its appended zero.

The only remaining choice is whether to use the reflection singleton.
Without it the cost is $B(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k)$; with it the
cost is $2+B(\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_k)$. Both constructions are
feasible, proving \cref{eq:real-nuclear-two-sums}.
\end{proof}

\begin{theorem}[The sharp additive gap between real and complex nuclear transport]
\label{thm:real-complex-nuclear-additive-gap}
Let $d_{\mathbb R,1}(P,Q)$ be the real nuclear transport minimum, and
let $d_{\mathbb C,1}(P,Q)$ be the minimum after complexifying the same
real subspaces. In every finite dimension,
\begin{equation}
 0\leq d_{\mathbb R,1}(P,Q)-d_{\mathbb C,1}(P,Q)
       \leq2-\sqrt3.
 \label{eq:real-complex-nuclear-additive-gap}
\end{equation}
The upper constant is attained by a single principal angle $\pi/3$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The complex minimum is $2\sum_i\sin\theta_i$ and is a lower bound
for the real one. For an angle list $\theta$, write their difference
as $D_k(\theta)$. The matching formula makes $D_k$ continuous on the
compact ordered simplex
$\pi/2\geq\theta_1\geq\cdots\geq\theta_k\geq0$.
Its maximum on the boundary is bounded by a maximum with fewer angles:
a zero angle can be discarded, a right angle can use a reflection of
cost two, and two equal adjacent angles can be coupled at cost
$4\sin\theta_i$. Each removed block attains its complex minimum, so
removing it cannot decrease the largest possible gap.

We show that when $k\geq2$ there is no interior local maximum.
Let $C_0=B(\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_k)$ and
$C_1=2+B(\theta_2,\ldots,\theta_k)$ be the two costs in
\cref{eq:real-nuclear-two-sums}, and put
\[
 A=C_0-2\sum_i\sin\theta_i,\qquad
 B=C_1-2\sum_i\sin\theta_i.
\]
Thus $D_k=\min\{A,B\}$. At a strictly ordered interior point,
\[
 \partial_1A=2\cos\frac{\theta_1+\theta_2}{2}
                    -2\cos\theta_1>0,
 \qquad \partial_1B=-2\cos\theta_1<0.
\]
A local maximum must therefore satisfy $A=B$.
The tie set is a smooth hypersurface, since
$\partial_1(A-B)=2\cos((\theta_1+\theta_2)/2)>0$.
The Lagrange multiplier condition on this hypersurface gives
$\lambda\in(0,1)$ such that
\[
 \nabla F=0,\qquad F=\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B.
\]
The interval for $\lambda$ follows from the two opposite first
derivatives just displayed.

For each coordinate $i$, let $q_i^0$ and $q_i^1$ be the cosine of its
paired half-sum in $C_0$ and $C_1$. A canonical singleton has half-sum
$\theta_i/2$, and the reflection singleton in $C_1$ has $q_1^1=0$.
The stationary equations are
\[
 \cos\theta_i=\lambda q_i^0+(1-\lambda)q_i^1.
\]
The two cosines differ at every coordinate: the two pairings use the
opposite adjacent neighbors, with the zero and reflection conventions
at the ends. Strict concavity of $q\mapsto\sqrt{1-q^2}$ on $[0,1]$
therefore gives
\[
 s_i:=\lambda\sqrt{1-(q_i^0)^2}
        +(1-\lambda)\sqrt{1-(q_i^1)^2}
       <\sin\theta_i.
\]

The Hessian of $F$ is positive definite. Indeed, a paired cost contributes
$-\sin((\theta_i+\theta_j)/2)(v_i+v_j)^2$ to its second variation;
a canonical singleton contributes $-\sin(\theta_i/2)v_i^2$;
and a reflection contributes zero. The subtracted complex minimum
contributes $2\sum_i\sin\theta_i v_i^2$.
Using $(v_i+v_j)^2\leq2(v_i^2+v_j^2)$, and weakening the singleton
terms to the same bound, yields
\[
 v^T\nabla^2F\,v
       \geq2\sum_i(\sin\theta_i-s_i)v_i^2>0
       \quad(v\ne0).
\]
Since $k\geq2$, the tie hypersurface has a nonzero tangent vector.
Along a smooth curve in that direction its second variation is positive:
on the tie set $D_k=F$, and $\nabla F=0$ removes the curve's
acceleration term. This contradicts a local maximum.

Compactness and induction now reduce the maximal gap to one angle.
There it equals
\[
 \min\{4\sin(\theta/2)-2\sin\theta,\ 2-2\sin\theta\}.
\]
The first expression increases on $[0,\pi/2]$, the second decreases,
and they agree at $\theta=\pi/3$. Their common value is
$2-\sqrt3$, which proves both the bound and sharpness.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[When the real nuclear minimum equals the complex one]
\label{cor:real-nuclear-parity}
The real nuclear transport minimum equals $2\sum_i\sin\theta_i$
exactly when each positive acute principal angle has even multiplicity.
Right-angle multiplicities are unrestricted.
The real canonical rotation minimizes the nuclear cost exactly when
there is at most one positive principal angle and that angle is at most
$\pi/3$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
For $0<\theta<\pi/2$, both choices in
$s_1(\theta)=\min\{4\sin(\theta/2),2\}$ strictly exceed
$2\sin\theta$. At a right angle the reflection attains equality.
Also
\[
 e_1(\theta,\psi)
 =4\sin\frac{\theta+\psi}{2}
 \geq2\sin\theta+2\sin\psi,
\]
with equality for positive angles exactly when $\theta=\psi$.
Equality in the finite matching sum therefore requires and permits
pairing each positive acute angle with an equal angle. The complex
minimum is the displayed sine sum.

If there are two positive angles, their paired cost is strictly less
than the sum of their canonical costs, since
\[
 4\sin\frac{\theta+\psi}{2}
 <4\sin\frac\theta2+4\sin\frac\psi2.
\]
For a single positive angle, the one-plane canonical choice is optimal
exactly when $4\sin(\theta/2)\leq2$, or $\theta\leq\pi/3$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The two largest angles determine canonical optimality]
\label{cor:real-smoothed-canonical-top-two}
For the Charbonnier cost, let $\theta_{(1)}\geq\theta_{(2)}$ be the
two largest principal angles, inserting zeros when necessary.
The canonical rotation is globally optimal over the real field exactly
when
\[
 \cos\theta_{(1)}\geq\tfrac12\eta^{-2},\qquad
 \tan\frac{\theta_{(1)}}2\tan\frac{\theta_{(2)}}2
       \leq\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^2+4}}.
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The matching formula shows that canonical transport is optimal exactly
when neither a single reflection nor a paired rotation lowers its cost.
By \cref{cor:real-four-smoothed}, these conditions are
\[
 \cos\theta_i\geq\tfrac12\eta^{-2},\qquad
 \frac{\cos((\theta_i+\theta_j)/2)}
      {\cos((\theta_i-\theta_j)/2)}\geq\eta^{-2}
 \quad(i\ne j).
\]
Writing $u_i=\tan(\theta_i/2)$ makes the ratio
$(1-u_iu_j)/(1+u_iu_j)$. Its inequality becomes
\[
 u_iu_j\leq\frac{1-\eta^{-2}}{1+\eta^{-2}}
           =\frac{\tau}{\sqrt{\tau^2+4}}.
\]
All $u_i$ are nonnegative and increase with the angle, so the two largest
angles give the strongest pair condition. The largest angle also gives
the strongest reflection condition.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Canonical real Schatten transport from the two largest angles]
\label{cor:real-schatten-canonical-top-two}
Let $1<p<2$, and let $\theta_{(1)}\geq\theta_{(2)}$ be the two largest
principal angles, inserting zeros when necessary. Put
\[
 c_p=\arccos\frac{2-p}{p},\qquad
 r_p=2\arcsin(2^{-1/p}).
\]
For $c_p<t\leq\pi/2$, let $a_p(t)\in(0,c_p)$ be the unique smaller
root of
\[
 (2\sin(a_p(t)/2))^{p-1}\cos(a_p(t)/2)
       =(2\sin(t/2))^{p-1}\cos(t/2).
\]
The canonical real transport minimizes the Schatten $p$-cost exactly when
\[
 \theta_{(1)}\leq r_p
 \quad\hbox{and}\quad
 \left[\theta_{(1)}\leq c_p
       \ \hbox{or}\ \theta_{(2)}\leq a_p(\theta_{(1)})\right].
\]
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
The amplitude $g_p(t)=(2\sin(t/2))^{p-1}\cos(t/2)$ strictly
increases from zero to its maximum at $c_p$, then strictly decreases
to zero at $\pi$. This proves existence and uniqueness of $a_p(t)$.
A single reflection cannot improve the canonical cost exactly when
$2(2\sin(\theta/2))^p\leq2^p$, or $\theta\leq r_p$.

For two distinct positive angles $\theta>\psi$, put
$h=(\theta+\psi)/2$ and $d=(\theta-\psi)/2$.
The restricted scalar cost has its canonical endpoint at $d$, where
its derivative is $p[g_p(\theta)-g_p(\psi)]$.
The scalar cost strictly decreases to its unique minimizer and strictly
increases afterward. Thus a pair improves the canonical cost exactly
when $g_p(\theta)<g_p(\psi)$.
Equal angles improve it exactly when they exceed $c_p$.
Pairs involving zero cannot improve it.

If $\theta_{(1)}\leq c_p$, every pair is subcritical and canonical.
If $\theta_{(1)}>c_p$, the pairs containing $\theta_{(1)}$ are all
canonical exactly when every other angle is at most
$a_p(\theta_{(1)})$. In that case the remaining angles are below
$c_p$, so their mutual pairs are canonical as well. Conversely, if
$\theta_{(2)}>a_p(\theta_{(1)})$, the largest pair strictly improves
the cost, including the case of equal largest angles. The matching
formula now proves the criterion.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Sharp real Schatten rounding constant]
\label{cor:real-schatten-rounding-constant}
For $1\leq p\leq2$, every real orthogonal matrix $U$ and real
orthogonal projection $P$ admit a real orthogonal $V$ commuting with
$P$ such that
\begin{equation}
 \|U-V\|_p\leq
    \frac{\|[P,U]\|_p}{\sqrt{1-2^{-2/p}}}.
 \label{eq:real-schatten-rounding-constant}
\end{equation}
The constant is optimal in every ambient dimension at least two.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Put $u=\sin(\theta/2)$ for a principal angle, so
$0\leq u\leq1/\sqrt2$. The best one-plane real transporter has
Schatten power $\min\{2(2u)^p,2^p\}$, while the corresponding
commutator block has power $2(2u\sqrt{1-u^2})^p$.
For $u>0$ their ratio of norms is
\[
 \begin{cases}
  (1-u^2)^{-1/2},&u\leq2^{-1/p},\\[2pt]
  \bigl(2^{1/p}u\sqrt{1-u^2}\bigr)^{-1},&u\geq2^{-1/p}.
 \end{cases}
\]
The first branch increases in $u$; the second decreases on
$[2^{-1/p},1/\sqrt2]$, since $u\sqrt{1-u^2}$ increases there.
Their common maximum is $(1-2^{-2/p})^{-1/2}$.
At $u=0$ both block costs vanish. Choosing the cheaper one-plane
transporter independently and summing the $p$th powers gives
\cref{eq:real-schatten-rounding-constant} through the transporter--rounding
bijection.

For sharpness, take a single principal plane with
$\theta=2\arcsin(2^{-1/p})$. Every real line transporter on this
plane is a canonical or long rotation or a reflection, and the
canonical and reflection choices have the same cost. Their ratio to
the commutator cost is the displayed constant. Adding fixed common
directions preserves this rank-one minimum, by the matching formula
(or its nuclear endpoint). Hence every ambient dimension at least two
has the same optimal constant.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Explicit matching costs at exponent three halves]
\label{cor:real-three-halves-matching}
For $\rho(s)=s^{3/2}$, put $f(t)=(2\sin(t/2))^{3/2}$.
For a pair $\theta\geq\psi$, set
$h=(\theta+\psi)/2$, $d=(\theta-\psi)/2$, and $c=\cos h$.
The matching weights are
\[
 s_{3/2}(\theta)=\min\{2f(\theta),2^{3/2}\},
\]
\[
 e_{3/2}(\theta,\psi)=
 \begin{cases}
  2f(\theta)+2f(\psi),&
       c\geq\dfrac{\cos d}{1+2\cos d},\\[3pt]
  2\sqrt{8(1+c)\sqrt{1-2c}},&
       c<\dfrac{\cos d}{1+2\cos d}.
 \end{cases}
\]
On the second branch an attaining common rotation has angle
\[
 \gamma=\arccos\frac{c}{(1-2c)\cos d}.
\]
Write $x_i=\sin(\theta_{(i)}/2)$ for the two largest angles, inserting
zeros when necessary. The real canonical rotation is globally optimal
exactly when
\begin{equation}
 x_1\leq2^{-2/3},\qquad x_1^2+x_1x_2+x_2^2\leq1.
 \label{eq:real-three-halves-canonical-top-two}
\end{equation}
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
Above its scalar transition, the optimal phase satisfies
$\cos\delta_{3/2}(h)=c/(1-2c)$, and the squared scalar minimum is
$8(1+c)\sqrt{1-2c}$, by
\cref{eq:schatten-three-halves-phase,eq:schatten-three-halves-value}.
The restricted optimum is canonical exactly when
$\delta_{3/2}(h)\leq d$, which is equivalent to
$c\geq\cos d/(1+2\cos d)$. This inequality also includes every
subcritical $h$, since then $c\geq1/3$. Substituting the scalar minimum
gives the second branch and the attaining rotation. On that branch
$c<1/3$, so its denominator is positive.

A single reflection improves canonical transport exactly when
$2(2\sin(\theta/2))^{3/2}>2^{3/2}$.
Its exclusion for every angle is the first inequality in
\cref{eq:real-three-halves-canonical-top-two}.
For $x=\sin(\theta/2)$ and $y=\sin(\psi/2)$, direct expansion gives
\[
 \cos h\,(1+2\cos d)-\cos d
       =2(1-x^2-xy-y^2).
\]
Thus no pair lowers the canonical cost exactly when
$x^2+xy+y^2\leq1$ for every pair. The expression increases with either
nonnegative argument, so the two largest angles suffice. Apply
\cref{thm:real-matching-minimum}.
\end{proof}
