## PfSylvester: James Joseph Sylvester

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

James Joseph Sylvester lived from 1814 to 1897. His career did not follow a smooth university path. He took the Cambridge mathematical examinations but, as a Jewish student unwilling to subscribe to the required religious articles, could not receive a Cambridge degree at that time. He later worked in insurance and studied law. His friendship with Arthur Cayley developed while both were working in the legal world. He also held university posts, including at Johns Hopkins and Oxford. These circumstances matter because a theorem name can hide the institutions and exclusions through which a person actually worked.

<!-- beat -->

For our mathematical story, Sylvester is a central character rather than a footnote to Rosenblum. His 1884 publication on the matrix equation p x equals x q belongs to the algebraic background of equations now called Sylvester equations. Modern notation often writes A X minus X B equals Y. We should distinguish that later inhomogeneous form and its operator estimates from the exact statement of the nineteenth-century paper. In our perturbation lectures, this family of equations is a bridge from a residual to an unwanted subspace component. Sylvester gives that bridge a visible human and historical starting point.

## PfJordan: Camille Jordan

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Camille Jordan lived from 1838 to 1922. He trained at the École Polytechnique and worked as an engineer while carrying out substantial mathematical research. Later he held professorships in Paris. His work crossed algebra, geometry, and analysis: a student might meet his name in group theory, a matrix normal form, or a theorem about plane curves. That range is useful context. The Jordan in our subspace history is a person with a broad mathematical career, not simply the label on one theorem.

<!-- beat -->

The publication we follow here is his 1875 Essay on Geometry in n Dimensions. It belongs to the early history of comparing higher-dimensional subspaces through angles. Two lines can be compared by one angle, but two larger spaces can agree in some directions and differ in others. The resulting list of principal angles will become one of the basic objects in the Davis–Kahan story. This is one selected thread from Jordan’s work. It does not imply that every modern projection formula or every later perturbation estimate was already present in his paper.

## PfRayleigh: John William Strutt · Lord Rayleigh

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Lord Rayleigh was John William Strutt, who lived from 1842 to 1919. Rayleigh is a title, not a different collaborator’s surname. He worked across mathematical and experimental physics, with a deep interest in waves and sound. He became Cavendish Professor at Cambridge and received the 1904 Nobel Prize in Physics for work connected with the discovery of argon. The breadth is worth remembering: the name attached to a variational quotient belongs to someone whose scientific work also depended on physical apparatus and observation.

<!-- beat -->

The Theory of Sound, whose first volume appeared in 1877, gives us a natural setting for eigenproblems. A vibrating system has characteristic frequencies and shapes of motion. Exact solutions may be difficult, so one tries simpler descriptions. The Rayleigh quotient is one way to turn a proposed direction or mode shape into an eigenvalue estimate. Our later lectures ask how to certify such approximations and distinguish the stability of a frequency from the stability of its associated direction. Rayleigh’s physics helps explain why these mathematical questions are worth asking.

## PfRitz: Walter (Walther) Ritz

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Walter Ritz, also written Walther, lived from 1878 to 1909. He was a Swiss theoretical physicist who studied in Zürich and Göttingen and worked on spectroscopy as well as variational problems. His life was cut short at thirty-one. It is easy to encounter Rayleigh–Ritz as a single technical phrase and imagine a single joint episode. The two names instead refer to different people, different careers, and separate publications. Keeping the people visible helps prevent a modern compound name from becoming an inaccurate story about collaboration.

<!-- beat -->

Ritz’s 1909 paper develops an approximation method for variational problems in mathematical physics. Rather than solve the full problem at once, choose a simpler family of possible functions and optimize within that family. In a matrix setting, a trial subspace plays a related role. This gives the later perturbation theory something practical to analyze: what does a computed approximation tell us about the exact spectral space? The person behind the name was working between mathematics and physics; the reusable lesson is how a tractable approximation can become a mathematical object whose error we can investigate.

## PfWeyl: Hermann Weyl

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Hermann Weyl lived from 1885 to 1955. He studied under David Hilbert in Göttingen and later worked at ETH Zürich and the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. His research connected geometry, analysis, and mathematical physics. In this series, Weyl appears through eigenvalue theory, but that is only one part of his mathematical life. The same person belongs to the histories of manifolds, symmetry, and quantum theory. A portrait helps us recognize that these are intersecting strands of a career rather than unrelated entries in a glossary.

<!-- beat -->

Our publication landmark is his 1912 paper in Mathematische Annalen, in the history of eigenvalue distribution and inequalities. The conceptual distinction to keep is simple: an eigenvalue is a number, while an eigenspace is a collection of directions. Bounds on the movement of the numbers do not automatically control a chosen basis, especially near repeated eigenvalues. This distinction makes room for the later Davis–Kahan angle theorems. Weyl’s role in the lecture is therefore both an individual research story and a reminder to ask exactly which mathematical object a stability theorem controls.

Rayleigh and Ritz illustrate why a compound theorem name needs care. The two people had separate careers and publications. Sylvester and Cayley illustrate a different situation: an actual intellectual friendship. Jordan’s engineering career and Weyl’s movement among several mathematical subjects show further ways a research life can cross boundaries. None of those facts proves a theorem. They help us avoid imagining a history made only of isolated names and a perfectly straight chain of discoveries.
