This lecture meets five people behind operator theory and reliable numerical computation. Their careers include teaching, books, migration, political conflict, and collaborations. The shared question is how to compare mathematical structures when an operator changes, but these people did not all approach it from the same direction. We will connect each biography to one selected publication. This is a standalone introduction, so recognizing the earlier history lecture is helpful but not required.

## PoSznagy: Béla Sz.-Nagy

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Béla Sz.-Nagy is the abbreviated form of Béla Szőkefalvi-Nagy. He lived from 1913 to 1998 and was closely associated with Szeged, where he taught and helped build an influential mathematical community. With Frigyes Riesz he wrote a widely used functional analysis text. Writing and teaching are part of the history here: later researchers inherit ways of organizing a subject as well as isolated results. The longer name on a photograph and the shorter name in a reference can otherwise look like two different people.

<!-- beat -->

His 1947 work on perturbations of self-adjoint transformations includes the nearby-projection construction in our historical account. A projection records a whole subspace without choosing a basis. A suitable length-preserving map can then identify two nearby spaces. This is one of the tools behind later direct-rotation and perturbation arguments. We will not derive its formula in this biography lecture. The point is to attach the idea to a person, a place of mathematical activity, and a publication, while remembering the wider role of teaching and shared texts in making the subject usable.

## PoHalmos: Paul Richard Halmos

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Paul Halmos lived from 1916 to 2006. Born in Budapest, he moved to the United States as a child and built a career spanning several universities. Alongside research, he became known for mathematical exposition and textbooks. He also photographed mathematicians. That last detail is particularly fitting for a lecture that puts faces beside theorem names. Clear writing, teaching, and visual records help preserve a community’s understanding, even though none of them is a substitute for the mathematics itself.

<!-- beat -->

The publication we use is Two Subspaces, which appeared in 1969. It organizes the geometry of two closed subspaces through their orthogonal projections. Shared directions and the genuinely interacting part should be distinguished rather than treated as one opaque matrix. In finite dimensions, principal-plane pictures recover familiar two-dimensional geometry. Halmos’s account belongs to a longer history that includes Jordan, Dixmier, and Davis. Keeping those earlier contributors visible avoids letting the best-known later exposition absorb the whole credit for the subject.

## PoKato: Tosio Kato

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Tosio Kato lived from 1917 to 1999. He trained in physics at Tokyo, and the war disrupted the early stages of his career. His later work crossed mathematical physics, differential equations, and functional analysis. He moved to Berkeley in 1962. His 1966 book Perturbation Theory for Linear Operators became a central reference for the subject. This is a reminder that an operator-theory name can come from someone whose original questions were physical, and whose career crossed countries and disciplinary boundaries.

<!-- beat -->

In our series, Kato helps explain why one follows spectral projections rather than an arbitrary eigenbasis. An isolated part of the spectrum defines a whole subspace, and a suitable transformation can compare that space as the operator varies. His nearby-projection construction builds on earlier work, including Sz.-Nagy’s. His book also treats settings beyond finite matrices, where domains and unbounded operators matter. The photograph is from 1988, much later than the first edition: it identifies the person, not what he looked like at the moment the book was written.

## PoDavis: Chandler Davis

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

Chandler Davis lived from 1926 to 2022. He was a mathematician and writer whose life was also shaped by political conflict. He refused to cooperate with the House Un-American Activities Committee, was dismissed from the University of Michigan, and served a prison sentence. He later worked at the University of Toronto and continued to advocate for academic freedom. These are documented parts of the person’s history, not mathematical assumptions or explanations of why a particular theorem is true.

<!-- beat -->

His mathematical path includes Separation of Two Linear Subspaces in 1958 and the first two Rotation of Eigenvectors papers in 1963 and 1965. The familiar Davis–Kahan name therefore should not hide Davis’s earlier work. Comparing spaces, finding canonical motions between them, and controlling their rotation were already active questions in his research. The joint announcement in 1969 and the extended 1970 paper with William Kahan brought further quantitative results. We can recognize the collaboration more accurately when we first recognize the distinct career and prior contributions of each collaborator.

## PoKahan: William Morton Kahan

Why: A life, a publication, and a mathematical connection.
Recall: The portrait identifies the person; the publication fixes the historical anchor.

William Morton Kahan was born in Toronto in 1933. His career is closely associated with numerical analysis, floating-point computation, and Berkeley. He received the ACM Turing Award for 1989 for fundamental contributions to numerical analysis. The human thread to remember is sustained attention to whether a computation deserves our trust. Producing digits is not enough. The mathematical problem may itself be sensitive, and arithmetic on a machine introduces its own questions. Those concerns are related, but should not be collapsed into one explanation of every numerical error.

<!-- beat -->

With Chandler Davis, Kahan published The Rotation of Eigenvectors by a Perturbation, Part Three, in 1970. The paper gives angle bounds for spectral subspaces under specified hypotheses and discusses direct rotations and their optimality. It belongs to the intersection of operator theory and numerical analysis that this lecture series explores. The selected photo was taken in 2008, not in 1970. A portrait and a publication card serve different purposes: one helps us meet the person, while the other fixes the date and scope of the mathematical work being discussed.

## PoConnections: Different lives, intersecting questions

Why: Distinguish collaboration, parallel work, and later reuse.
Recall: A shared mathematical topic alone does not establish a personal connection.

The careers just introduced bring together several ways of contributing. Sz.-Nagy helped build a teaching community and shared texts. Halmos made structure and exposition memorable. Kato connected operator theory with mathematical physics. Davis combined research with writing and public commitments, and Kahan pursued trustworthy computation. The Davis–Kahan paper is a specific collaboration within that larger environment. It does not mean every tool in the paper originated with its two authors, or that everyone on this slide was directly collaborating with everyone else.

## PoSummary: Faces, questions, and the next lectures

Recall: This episode is a selected group, not an exhaustive history.

Kahan connected numerical analysis with the reliability of computation. These connections organize the stories just introduced. The main memory should be a connection between a person and a question, not a list of awards. How can projections identify moving spaces? How can a structural description simplify two subspaces? How do physical problems lead to operator theory? What was already in Davis’s research before the joint paper? Why does numerical reliability require mathematical sensitivity analysis? Those are useful questions to carry into the technical lectures. Rosenblum, Dixmier, the singular-value researchers, and YWS remain visible in our coverage notes and the broader historical overview.
