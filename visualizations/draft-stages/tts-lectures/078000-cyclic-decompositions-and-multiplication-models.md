# Turning an operator into multiplication by its spectral variable

The spectral theorem describes a self-adjoint operator through a projection-valued measure. There is also a concrete coordinate model: on a suitable space of square-integrable functions, the operator acts by multiplication by the spectral variable. The measure determines which functions and which multipliers are allowed.

For example, multiplication by t on the square-integrable functions over the unit interval has continuous spectrum and no eigenvectors. The multiplication-operator model extends to general self-adjoint operators when one allows an orthogonal sum of suitable L two spaces.

The construction begins with a single vector. From it, build the smallest closed space containing every vector obtained by applying a bounded Borel function of A. This is called the cyclic subspace generated by the vector.

## The spectral orbit of one vector

Fix a vector x in H. Consider all vectors of the form f of A applied to x, where f ranges over bounded Borel functions. Take the closure of their linear span. Call the resulting closed subspace K sub x.

It contains x because the constant-one function acts as the identity. It is invariant under every bounded Borel function of A: applying g of A to f of A times x gives the vector associated with the product g times f.

Because the calculus is closed under complex conjugation, K sub x is also invariant under adjoints of all these operators. Thus it is a reducing subspace. Both the subspace and its orthogonal complement are preserved by A and by its Borel calculus.

Why does closure matter? A sequence of bounded Borel functions can approach an unbounded L two function in mean-square sense. The limiting vector may belong to the cyclic subspace even though no single bounded symbol produces it directly.

## A natural map from functions to vectors

The vector x has a finite positive scalar spectral measure mu sub x. Take a bounded Borel function f and map it to f of A applied to x.

Compute the squared norm of this vector. Using the adjoint law and the product law of functional calculus, the squared norm equals the inner product of x with the operator corresponding to the squared magnitude of f, applied to x.

The spectral-integral formula turns that inner product into the integral of the squared magnitude of f against mu sub x.

But that integral is precisely the squared L two norm of f.

So our map from bounded Borel functions to H preserves norms, provided functions that agree almost everywhere for mu sub x are identified. It also preserves inner products by polarization, making it an isometry on its initial domain.

That one identity is the engine of the multiplication model.

## Completing the map

Bounded measurable functions are dense in L two of a finite measure. To verify this, first truncate the absolute magnitude of an arbitrary L two function at a large finite threshold. The truncated functions converge in L two by dominated convergence, since their squared errors are bounded by the original integrable squared magnitude.

Therefore our isometry on bounded symbols extends uniquely by completeness to an isometric linear map from all of L two of mu sub x into H.

The range of an isometry from a complete space is closed. The range contains every f of A times x for bounded Borel f, and those vectors are dense in the cyclic subspace by definition. Hence the extended range is exactly K sub x.

We have proved that the cyclic subspace is isometrically equivalent to L two of its scalar spectral measure.

## Intertwining the operator

An isometry of spaces would be interesting by itself, but we need it to preserve the operator's action.

Under the map sending f to f of A times x, what does A do? Since the function lambda represents A under functional calculus, A applied to f of A times x equals the vector associated with lambda times f.

Thus the model of A on L two is multiplication by lambda. For a bounded A, the spectral coordinate lambda is bounded on the spectral support, so multiplication by lambda is a bounded operator on L two.

The map intertwines the original operator with multiplication: apply A after moving from L two to H, or multiply by lambda first and then move to H, and the results agree.

In the complex normal case, the coordinate can be a complex spectral value rather than a real number. For a self-adjoint operator it is real, and the multiplication operator is self-adjoint.

## Why one vector may not suffice

Take A to be the identity on a two-dimensional Hilbert space. For any nonzero x, every f of A applied to x equals f of one times x. The cyclic subspace generated by x is only its one-dimensional span.

No single vector generates the whole two-dimensional space. This is not a failure of the construction. It tells us that the eigenvalue one occurs with multiplicity two, and one copy of a scalar L two space cannot record both independent directions.

A second orthogonal cyclic vector supplies a second copy. The whole space becomes the orthogonal sum of two one-dimensional multiplication models, with both copies multiplying by one.

For a more substantial example, take two copies of L two of the unit interval, and let A multiply by t in each copy. One vector might generate the first copy, while another generates the second. Their scalar measures may be identical, but the two copies represent independent multiplicity.

This illustrates why spectral measures alone, without recording multiplicity, do not completely classify a general self-adjoint operator.

## Constructing orthogonal cyclic pieces

How do we decompose an arbitrary Hilbert space into such pieces? Start with one nonzero vector and its closed cyclic subspace. Because that subspace reduces A, its orthogonal complement also reduces A. If the complement is nonzero, choose another vector there and construct its cyclic subspace. Continue.

In a finite-dimensional space this process terminates. In a separable infinite-dimensional space, an orthogonal family of nonzero closed subspaces must be countable. A careful maximality argument guarantees that choosing cyclic subspaces until no more are available gives a dense orthogonal sum.

Without separability, the family of summands may have an arbitrary index set. One proves existence using maximal orthogonal families: order the admissible collections by inclusion, take unions along chains, obtain a maximal collection, and observe that a nonzero vector orthogonal to all its cyclic subspaces would generate one more, contradicting maximality.

The orthogonal sum is complete because the orthogonal complement of the closed span is zero.

## The global model

Each cyclic component is isometric to an L two space over its scalar spectral measure. Putting the component isometries together produces an isometric equivalence between H and a Hilbert direct sum of these L two spaces.

Under this equivalence, A is coordinatewise multiplication by the spectral variable. Its spectral projections multiply each component by the indicator of the selected set. Its bounded Borel functions multiply by their scalar symbols.

This is the infinite-dimensional analogue of diagonalizing a matrix. The matrix diagonal entries have become a measurable coordinate, and repeated eigenvalues have become multiple L two summands.

## Two forms of spectral multiplicity

There is a distinction between an eigenvalue with several eigenvectors and a continuously distributed spectrum with several independent copies. In both cases, multiplicity counts how many independent channels carry the same spectral behavior.

An atom of a scalar measure represents an eigenvalue in its cyclic component. An absolutely continuous part can represent continuum spectral mass without eigenvectors. Singular continuous behavior is also possible; the multiplication model accommodates it because the measure need not have a density.

Therefore the model does not secretly assume every normal operator has an eigenbasis. It replaces that assumption with measurable functions and their L two geometry.

## A last calculation

Return to multiplication by t on L two of the unit interval. Choose x to be the constant-one function. Its scalar spectral measure is ordinary Lebesgue measure. If f is bounded, f of A applied to x is just the function f of t.

Bounded functions are dense in L two, so x is cyclic and this one component is the entire Hilbert space. This is the simplest possible instance of our theorem.

Now choose x to be a function that vanishes on the left half. Its scalar spectral measure ignores that half. Every f of A applied to x still vanishes there. The cyclic subspace generated by x cannot reach functions supported on the left half.

The cyclic subspace remembers not only the ambient spectrum but also what the chosen vector can see.

## The multiplicative spectral model

We have proved the norm-preserving correspondence between spectral symbols and a cyclic L two space, extended it by completeness, and explained the orthogonal decomposition of a general Hilbert space into cyclic pieces.

The multiplication model makes the rules of bounded Borel functional calculus concrete: multiplying two spectral symbols corresponds to composing their operators, complex conjugation corresponds to taking adjoints, and essential supremum controls operator norm. Convergence requires an appropriate bounded pointwise or dominated-convergence hypothesis on the spectral-measure side; pointwise convergence alone must not be confused with convergence in operator norm.
