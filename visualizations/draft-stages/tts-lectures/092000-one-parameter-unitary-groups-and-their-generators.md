# A motion that exists even when its velocity does not

Imagine an infinite collection of clocks. The first hand completes one revolution at some basic rate. The second runs twice as fast. The third runs three times as fast, and the rates continue increasing without bound.

Every individual hand moves smoothly. But if we regard the entire collection as one vector in an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, the combined motion raises a question. Does it make sense to differentiate that motion? Can there be a perfectly continuous evolution whose instantaneous velocity is undefined for some starting vectors?

Yes. This happens for unitary groups generated by unbounded self-adjoint operators. Stone's theorem tells us how the motion and its generator determine each other.

Let's construct the motion before stating the general theorem.

## An infinite collection of rotations

Work on the Hilbert space ell two. An element is an infinite sequence whose squared magnitudes have a finite sum. We use complex numbers so that rotation can be represented by multiplication by a complex phase.

Define A to multiply coordinate number N by N. This is a self-adjoint operator, but it is unbounded and it cannot act on every sequence in ell two.

Now choose a real time t. Define U at time t to multiply coordinate N by the complex exponential of imaginary N times t.

Every multiplier has absolute value one. So the squared length of every sequence is unchanged. U of t is therefore a unitary operator on the entire Hilbert space.

It also obeys the group law. If we evolve for time s and then for time t, the phases multiply, which adds their angles. The result is evolution for time s plus t. At time zero, every phase is one, so U of zero is the identity. Evolving for negative t undoes evolution for positive t.

Those are the algebraic ingredients of a one-parameter unitary group.

But continuity needs some care.

## Why the whole motion is continuous

Take any fixed sequence x in ell two. We want the evolved vector U of t applied to x to approach x as t approaches zero.

For the first ten coordinates, this is clear. There are only finitely many phases, and each phase approaches one.

What about all the remaining coordinates? Their angular speeds are not bounded. But the tail of a fixed square-summable sequence has arbitrarily small total squared length. And multiplying those tail coordinates by phases cannot increase their length.

Given a desired error, first choose a tail so small that it contributes almost nothing. Then choose time sufficiently close to zero to control the finitely many remaining coordinates.

Together, those two steps prove continuity on the whole Hilbert space.

This is strong continuity. We fix the vector first, and then let time approach zero.

If we instead asked for operator-norm continuity, we would be asking for one small time interval that works simultaneously for every unit vector. That fails.

To see it, take coordinate number N and set the time equal to pi divided by N. That coordinate rotates halfway around the circle and changes sign. The distance between its evolved basis vector and the original is two. But pi divided by N approaches zero as N grows.

Thus arbitrarily close to time zero, there is always some high-frequency direction that moves a full distance of two.

The motion is strongly continuous, but not continuous in operator norm.

## Does every vector have an instantaneous velocity?

Continuity says U of t x approaches x. Differentiability asks for more. It asks whether the difference between U of t x and x, divided by time, approaches some Hilbert-space vector.

Look at coordinate N. The complex phase is approximately one plus imaginary N times t for small time. So the difference quotient wants to multiply that coordinate by imaginary N.

For a sequence x, the candidate derivative therefore has N-th coordinate equal to imaginary N times the original N-th coordinate.

But this candidate belongs to ell two only if the sequence formed by multiplying coordinate N by N is square summable.

That is exactly the domain condition for A.

For instance, take x with N-th coordinate one divided by N squared. The candidate derivative has entries imaginary one divided by N, which form a square-summable sequence. This vector has a genuine derivative, and the derivative is imaginary A applied to x.

Now choose x with N-th coordinate one divided by N. This vector is in ell two, so U of t x is defined and continuous at zero. But the candidate derivative has every coordinate equal to the imaginary unit. That sequence is not square summable.

The difference quotient cannot converge to a vector in ell two, because convergence in ell two would force convergence of each coordinate to the same nonsquare-summable candidate.

Here is the distinction that Stone's theorem makes precise. The motion acts on every vector. Its infinitesimal generator acts only on the vectors whose motion is differentiable at time zero.

## Construct the group for a general self-adjoint operator

Our example used a diagonal operator. A general self-adjoint operator may have continuous spectrum, with no eigenvector basis at all.

The spectral theorem lets us use the same scalar phase rule. At each real spectral value s, attach the complex phase with angle t times s. Integrating that rule against the spectral resolution of A defines U of t.

The modulus of every phase is one, so U of t is unitary. Multiplying phases proves the group law. The scalar phases approach one as time approaches zero, and their distance from one is at most two. Because the spectral measure of a fixed vector has finite mass, dominated convergence proves strong continuity.

Notice that this argument needs no domain assumption on the vector. Every vector belongs to the domain of U of t.

Now take a vector in the domain of A. Divide the phase difference by imaginary time. At each spectral value, that quotient approaches s. Its magnitude is bounded by the magnitude of s.

The spectral-domain condition for A says precisely that the square of s is integrable against the vector's spectral measure. Dominated convergence therefore proves that the quotient converges in Hilbert-space norm to A applied to the vector.

So every domain vector has the expected derivative.

We can also prove the converse. Suppose that difference quotient converges in Hilbert-space norm for some vector. Its norms must remain bounded near zero. At each spectral value the squared magnitude of the quotient approaches s squared. Fatou's lemma then says that the spectral integral of s squared is finite.

But finiteness of that integral is exactly the condition for belonging to the domain of A.

We have recovered the whole domain, rather than just a subset of it, from differentiability of the motion.

## What Stone's theorem adds

So far we started with a self-adjoint operator A and constructed a strongly continuous unitary group U.

Stone's theorem also goes in the opposite direction. Start with any strongly continuous one-parameter unitary group. Define its infinitesimal generator by taking the limit of the difference quotient on precisely the vectors where that limit exists.

The theorem says that, with a consistent factor of the imaginary unit, the resulting operator is self-adjoint, and the original group is recovered by spectral exponentiation.

The first part of the converse is plausible from geometry. Unitarity preserves inner products. Differentiating that identity on sufficiently regular vectors gives symmetry of the generator.

But symmetry is weaker than self-adjointness for unbounded operators. To get the full domain, the proof uses the group to construct bounded inverses at nonreal shifts. Exponentially damping an integral of the group over positive time yields one resolvent; integrating over negative time yields another. These inverses establish the surjectivity conditions that upgrade symmetry to self-adjointness.

So the group contains enough information to recover not only the operator's action but also its correct domain.

There is a sign convention to fix. If we define U of t using the exponential of positive imaginary t times A, then dividing U of t x minus x by imaginary t gives A x. If we put a negative sign in the exponential, we must change the generator convention accordingly.

## Why this helps with unbounded equations

A self-adjoint generator A may not act on an arbitrary vector, but U of t acts on every vector and preserves its norm.

Suppose two different self-adjoint operators govern two spaces. We can apply the first unitary group, then a bounded map between the spaces, then the inverse of the second unitary group. This composition is bounded and well-defined for every input.

Such expressions are useful when constructing solutions of operator equations, including Sylvester equations. The bounded motions are easy to compose and estimate. When we differentiate them to recover an equation involving the generators, however, their restricted domains must be checked again.

That is the central balance: the unitary group gives an everywhere-defined motion, while the derivative of that motion remembers exactly where the unbounded generator is defined.

## Companion: mathematics and Lean correspondence (display only)

For self-adjoint \(A\), the Borel calculus defines \(U_A(t)=\int e^{it\lambda}\,dE_A(\lambda)\). Then \(U_A(s+t)=U_A(s)U_A(t)\), \(U_A(t)^*=U_A(-t)\), and \(t\mapsto U_A(t)x\) is norm-continuous for every \(x\in H\). Its generator, with the convention \((U_A(t)x-x)/(it)\), is \(A\) on precisely \(D(A)\). The forward derivative-domain implication uses dominated convergence; the converse uses Fatou's lemma. Stone's converse reconstructs a self-adjoint generator from a strongly continuous unitary group, using nonreal resolvents.
